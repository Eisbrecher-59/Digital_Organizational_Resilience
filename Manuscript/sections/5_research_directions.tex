\section{Research in Digital Resilience Management}
\label{sec:RD}

The preceding sections examined resilience as a cross-disciplinary construct spanning ecological, psychological, engineering, and organizational traditions. While these perspectives offer valuable insights, they also reveal persistent conceptual ambiguity, fragmented methodological approaches, and limited cumulative empirical validation. These weaknesses become particularly consequential in digitally mediated organizational environments characterized by adversarial dynamics, rapid technological change, and systemic interdependence.\\

This section therefore narrows the focus explicitly to digital organizational resilience -- and more specifically cyber resilience -- as the primary empirical and theoretical anchor for advancing resilience research in information systems. In digital contexts, resilience cannot remain a normative aspiration or a compliance-oriented checklist. It must be reconceptualized as a tractable socio-technical capability that can be observed, modeled, and governed under conditions of persistent and non-stationary disruption.\\

Conceptualizing organizational resilience in digitally mediated environments as observable through behavioral and computational indicators enables evaluable action. Understanding it as cumulative through iterative learning captures its path-dependent and developmental character. Embedding it within institutional and technological arrangements ensures that adaptive capacity is sustained over time rather than contingent on individual discretion. These shifts reposition organizational resilience in digital contexts as an enacted capability.\\
\\
Addressing the identified gaps requires a structured account of how resilience becomes enacted and at what levels adaptive capacity is distributed. We therefore propose an \emph{enactment–scope resilience framework} organized along two analytical dimensions.\\

The first dimension, enactment, specifies the mechanisms through which resilience moves from articulated intention to operational capability. As Section 3 demonstrated, resilience remains rhetorical when organizations lack observable indicators, governance structures that assign decision rights, and evaluative benchmarks that align incentives. Enactment thus captures the translation of resilience into measurable, designed, and governed socio-technical practice. Figure~\ref{fig:triangle} formalizes this dimension by identifying operational indicators, design principles, and benchmarks as the core mechanisms through which resilience becomes enacted.\\

The second dimension, scope, specifies where these mechanisms operate. Organizational resilience in digitally structured systems is inherently multi-level: adaptive capacity is distributed across micro-level routines, meso-level digital infrastructures, macro-level institutional arrangements, and inter-organizational ecosystems. Because digital systems couple actors, technologies, and governance structures across these levels, resilience cannot be understood at a single locus of control. Clarifying scope therefore addresses the empirical fragmentation identified earlier by specifying sites of adaptation and intervention.\\

Together, enactment and scope provide a structured response to the three research gaps identified in Section \ref{sec:RG}. Enactment reduces conceptual ambiguity by specifying observable mechanisms. Scope addresses empirical fragmentation by clarifying levels of analysis. Their integration enables methodological advancement by identifying measurable processes across distributed socio-technical systems. A complementary opportunity matrix (introduced later in this section) situates enactment mechanisms across organizational scopes and maps concrete research directions.\\
\\
IS research is uniquely positioned to advance this agenda because it examines digital infrastructures, platform governance, socio-technical coordination, and cross-boundary information flows. These traditions provide theoretical and methodological tools for analyzing how adaptive mechanisms are embedded in technological architectures and distributed across organizational levels. The enactment–scope framework therefore anchors the study of organizational resilience within digitally mediated systems within the core analytical strengths of the IS field.

\subsection{Operational Foundations of Resilience} 
A first research priority is to develop an operational foundation that makes resilience observable and tractable. Conceptual literature on resilience has emphasized that resilience is not simply robustness, redundancy, or agility, but rather an emergent capability that arises through the adaptive interplay of human, technological, and organizational components \autocite{ungar2021modeling, kalisch2024neurobiology}. This perspective aligns with long-standing systems theory traditions that conceive organizations as dynamic, adaptive systems \autocite{wiener1948cybernetics, holling1973resilience}. The challenge for information systems research is to move beyond metaphorical uses of resilience and specify the mechanisms through which it materializes in practice. Comparative work across domains has already shown how resilience emerges from the interaction of routines, infrastructures, and institutional processes \autocite{pumpuni2017resilience, manikis2019computational}, offering a foundation for cumulative theorization.\\

{\bf (I don't think the figure alone makes it concrete)} To make this shift concrete, Figure \ref{fig:triangle} introduces a framework that connects theory, measurement, and governance as the domains through which resilience becomes enacted capability. Each side of the triangle identifies the mechanisms that bridge these domains: \emph{operational indicators} make resilience mechanisms observable rather than symbolic, \emph{design principles} assign decision rights and structure adaptive responses, and \emph{benchmarks and warnings} align incentives and provide actionable signals. By combining these elements, the figure highlights how enacted resilience accumulates across contexts through iterative feedback and learning. In this way, the triangle provides a conceptual anchor for subsequent research directions by specifying not only what resilience is, but how it can be observed, designed, and cultivated.\\

One promising avenue is the identification of behavioral and computational indicators that allow resilience to be empirically grounded \autocite{kuypers2018designing, gillard2023efficient}. Indicators such as coordination speed, the capacity to reconfigure infrastructures, and the variability of responses under pressure can transform resilience from an aspirational idea into a measurable construct \autocite{brauer2008compartmental, mahajan2022participatory}. These measures enable organizations to benchmark their resilience and scholars to build cumulative knowledge across cases. The development of such indicators also requires a theoretical articulation of how individual and collective behaviors scale into system-level outcomes \autocite{luthar2000construct, rutter1987psychosocial}. Emerging approaches in behavioral analytics and AI-assisted modeling provide opportunities to calibrate resilience indicators against observable signatures in digital traces \autocite{rane2024artificial}. This task is particularly urgent in digital contexts where disruptions unfold rapidly and interactions are highly distributed.\\

Computational approaches, including agent-based modeling and other forms of simulation, are particularly well suited for probing how resilience emerges from micro-level dynamics. They enable researchers to test how different coordination patterns, incentive structures, or infrastructural configurations affect the ability of organizations to absorb and adapt to disruption \autocite{gillard2023efficient, arnold2004informationsharing}. Such methods enable refinement of theory in light of simulated and empirical data, thereby bridging conceptual clarity with practical application. By operationalizing resilience in this way, IS research can help organizations move beyond rhetorical compliance and begin to cultivate resilience as a capability that can be systematically assessed, compared, and improved.

\subsection{Institutional and Cooperation Dynamics}

Resilience is shaped not only by organizational routines but also by the institutional environments and cooperative structures within which those routines are enacted. Mandates for resilience frequently result in rigid compliance activities rather than adaptive governance, as institutions define incentives, roles, and feedback loops that privilege procedural conformity over flexible response. Governance architectures can both enable and constrain resilience by creating ambiguity in decision rights, accountability gaps, and escalation failures, all of which limit adaptive performance \autocite{ostrom1990governing, anderies2004framework, lebel2006governance}. These institutional frictions manifest as role-based constraints, delayed responses, or reduced strategic options—dynamics that become most visible under conditions of stress. Addressing such limitations requires approaches that bridge institutional theory, regulatory ethnography, and actor–network perspectives with empirical fieldwork in digital infrastructures, thereby grounding abstract accounts of governance in the lived realities of resilience practice.\\

While Figure \ref{fig:triangle} establishes the mechanisms of enacted resilience, it does not specify the levels at which these mechanisms unfold. Figure \ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity} therefore extends the framework by situating research opportunities across \emph{micro-level routines}, \emph{meso-level infrastructures}, \emph{macro-level institutions}, and \emph{interorganizational ecosystems}. This opportunity matrix complements the triangle by clarifying not only \emph{how} resilience is enacted but also \emph{where} it can be studied and supported. It highlights how institutional frictions and cooperation dynamics manifest at meso and macro levels, while integrative opportunities exist across all four domains. By juxtaposing mechanisms (Figure \ref{fig:triangle}) with scopes (Figure \ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity}), the agenda specifies both the processes and contexts through which resilience can move from rhetoric to enactment.\\

Resilience also depends on how cooperation unfolds under pressure. Time-critical crises demand coordination across boundaries, yet trust asymmetries, activation delays, and incentive misalignments often undermine such cooperation. Theories of collective action, modular governance, and platform-mediated collaboration provide important starting points, especially in digital threat environments where rapid mobilization is essential \autocite{ostrom1990governing, brauer2008compartmental,mahajan2022participatory}. Empirical studies of modular security platforms such as MISP demonstrate how structural design can enable or inhibit timely collaboration, while participatory simulations and controlled experiments reveal thresholds for cooperative engagement and highlight potential structural failure points \autocite{gillard2023efficient, maillart2024computational}. Insights from disaster response research further show how information technology systems can support timely cross-organizational information-sharing, offering design principles transferable to adversarial digital contexts \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing}. Together, these perspectives highlight how institutional arrangements and cooperation dynamics jointly determine whether resilience emerges as a substantive capability or remains confined to symbolic compliance.

\subsection{Learning and Temporal Processes}

Resilience is not a fixed attribute but a cumulative capability that evolves over time through cycles of learning, organizational memory, and feedback. In practice, however, the temporal dimension of resilience remains under-theorized in IS scholarship, which tends to focus on single events or isolated organizational responses \autocite{luthar2000construct, rutter1987psychosocial}. A richer account requires situating resilience within developmental trajectories, tracing how organizations retain or lose adaptive capacity across successive disruptions. This means recognizing that resilience is shaped as much by what is forgotten as by what is remembered, and that learning is inherently uneven, contingent, and subject to erosion.\\

Insights from systems theory and ecology underscore this temporal perspective. Holling’s foundational work shows that resilience emerges from cycles of adaptation, in which growth, conservation, release, and reorganization follow one another in recurrent patterns \autocite{holling1973resilience}. Similarly, Wiener’s cybernetics highlights the role of feedback loops in stabilizing or destabilizing system behavior \autocite{wiener1948cybernetics}. Translated into organizational contexts, these frameworks suggest that resilience depends not only on the ability to respond to disruption but also on the capacity to reinforce effective responses, institutionalize lessons, and recalibrate strategies across time. In this sense, resilience can either accumulate through positive feedback and strategic reinforcement or decay through forgetting, inertia, and maladaptation.\\

Capturing these dynamics calls for methodological pluralism. Longitudinal case studies are essential to follow how organizations evolve in the wake of recurring disruptions, while process tracing can illuminate the causal mechanisms that link specific learning practices to resilience trajectories. Computational learning models provide complementary leverage, enabling researchers to simulate how different rates of forgetting, reinforcement, or cross-unit knowledge transfer affect long-term resilience outcomes. By foregrounding the temporal dimension of resilience, IS research can move beyond static definitions to theorize resilience as an evolving capability, one that waxes and wanes as organizations confront persistent or repeated disruption.

\subsection{Toward an Integrative Blueprint}

Resilience research cannot advance through fragmented perspectives alone; it requires an integrative framework that unifies behavioral, institutional, and temporal insights into a coherent sociotechnical account. Such integration is consistent with systems theory and ecological foundations that emphasize resilience as an emergent property shaped by feedback and adaptation \autocite{wiener1948cybernetics, holling1973resilience}. An integrative framework would formalize sociotechnical mechanisms by connecting the micro-level actions of individuals, the meso-level dynamics of organizations, and the macro-level constraints of governance regimes. Doing so also requires the development of indicators that move beyond compliance checklists and capture adaptation in real time, such as response variability, coordination under stress, and infrastructural reconfiguration \autocite{cutter2010disaster, aerts2018integrating, leslie2013response}. By grounding these measures in observable behavior, scholars can transform resilience into a tractable construct, bridging the persistent gap between principle and practice.\\

Concurrently, resilience must be understood as embedded within institutional and organizational contexts that condition what forms of adaptation are possible. Rules, incentives, and escalation pathways structure how resilience unfolds, and institutional analysis can help clarify how governance architectures both enable and constrain adaptive capacity \autocite{ungar2021modeling, ostrom1990governing}. Field-based and computational work already shows how such frictions manifest as delays, constrained options, or coordination failures, but much remains to be done to systematize these insights into generalizable frameworks. 
Integrating these institutional dynamics with behavioral and learning processes creates the foundation for a blueprint that captures resilience as both enacted practice and constrained possibility.\\

Figure \ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity} visualizes how these research opportunities distribute across organizational scopes, underscoring the need for integrative frameworks that bridge micro behaviors, meso infrastructures, macro institutions, and ecosystems.\\

Finally, Figures \ref{fig:triangle} and \ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity} together form the \emph{enactment–scope framework} that scaffolds this agenda. The triangle specifies the mechanisms through which resilience moves from rhetoric to enactment, while the opportunity matrix locates these mechanisms across organizational levels, clarifying where inquiry and intervention are most needed. An integrative framework that links theory with practice through multi-method programs of research—combining computational modeling, longitudinal case studies, and field-based validation—can generate standardized and transferable metrics of organizational resilience \autocite{gillard2023efficient, kalisch2024neurobiology,teece2007explicating}. Such efforts will not only enable cumulative theorizing within IS but also produce actionable guidance for managers and policymakers grappling with digital threats. By weaving together sociotechnical mechanisms, behavioral indicators, institutional constraints, and learning processes, the enactment–scope framework provides a coherent foundation for resilience scholarship \autocite{henfridsson2013generative, holling1973resilience}. It anchors resilience in mechanisms that are theoretically rigorous, empirically validated, and practically relevant, positioning IS research to lead the study of resilience in adversarial and digitally mediated environments where adaptation must occur in real time under conditions of uncertainty \autocite{weick2011managing, woods2015four}.
