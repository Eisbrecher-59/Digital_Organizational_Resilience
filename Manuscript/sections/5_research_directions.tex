The Enactment--Scope framework is useful only if it generates concrete research questions. This section develops that next step. The focus here is digital organizational resilience, and especially cyber resilience, because digital environments make the core analytical problems unusually visible. In such settings, resilience cannot be treated as a slogan or as a compliance label attached after the fact. It has to be studied as a socio-technical capability that is built, enacted, and tested under conditions of persistent disruption.\\

This shift changes the object of inquiry. Once resilience is treated as something that can be observed in behavior, infrastructure, and governance arrangements, it becomes possible to evaluate it rather than merely invoke it. Once it is treated as cumulative rather than static, questions of learning, memory, reinforcement, and erosion move to the center. And once it is situated in digitally mediated environments, adaptive capacity can no longer be reduced to managerial discretion or organizational culture alone. It also depends on architectures, platforms, institutional settings, and the ways these elements condition what forms of response are possible.\\

The research agenda that follows is organized around two linked questions. The first concerns \emph{enactment}: through what mechanisms does resilience move from stated intention to operational capability? The second concerns \emph{scope}: at what organizational and institutional levels do these mechanisms actually operate? These questions follow directly from the earlier diagnosis of resilience research. Resilience remains rhetorical when organizations lack observable indicators, governance structures that allocate decision authority, and evaluative benchmarks that align action with adaptive goals. At the same time, resilience in digitally mediated environments is distributed. It is not located in one team, one platform, or one formal rule. It is produced across routines, infrastructures, institutional arrangements, and inter-organizational ecosystems.\\

This is also where information systems research has a distinctive advantage. The field already addresses digital infrastructures, platform governance, socio-technical coordination, and cross-boundary information flows. These are not peripheral concerns here. They are the substance of the problem. The sections below therefore develop three research directions: operationalizing resilience enactment, examining resilience across organizational and institutional scopes, and tracing how resilience evolves over time.

\subsection{Operationalizing Resilience Enactment}

A first priority is to make resilience observable in practice. That is the central task of the enactment dimension of the framework. Organizations frequently invoke resilience in strategic language, governance documents, and policy discourse, yet it often remains unclear how resilience should actually be identified during disruption. IS research is well placed to address that problem because it can connect behavioral indicators, computational traces, and organizational processes in ways that make adaptive capacity empirically visible.\\
\\
The challenge is to move beyond metaphor. It is no longer enough to say that resilience matters. Research has to specify how resilience appears in practice and how it can be distinguished from symbolic claims about preparedness or adaptability. Comparative work across domains already suggests that resilience emerges through the interaction of routines, infrastructures, and institutional processes \autocite{pumpuni2017resilience,manikis2019computational}. What is still missing is a more systematic basis for turning those observations into measures and design principles that organizations can actually use.\\

Figure \ref{fig:triangle} is intended to support that move. It connects theory, measurement, and governance as the three domains through which resilience becomes enacted capability. Each side of the triangle captures a different mechanism of translation. \emph{Operational indicators} make resilience observable rather than merely symbolic. \emph{Design principles} allocate decision rights and structure adaptive responses. \emph{Benchmarks and warnings} align incentives while producing actionable signals. The figure matters less as a visual device than as an analytical claim: resilience becomes governable only when these domains are linked rather than treated separately.\\

One promising line of work concerns the development of behavioral and computational indicators that make resilience empirically assessable \autocite{kuypers2018designing,gillard2023efficient}. Indicators such as coordination speed, the ability to reconfigure infrastructures, and the variability of response under pressure can help turn resilience from an aspirational concept into a measurable one \autocite{mahajan2022participatory}. They also make comparison possible. Organizations can benchmark themselves, and researchers can begin to accumulate evidence across cases. Yet the development of such indicators is not simply a technical exercise. It also depends on a theoretical account of how behaviors at individual and collective levels scale into system-level outcomes \autocite{luthar2000construct}.\\

Digital environments make this task especially urgent. Disruptions unfold quickly, interactions are distributed, and many adaptive signals are embedded in digital traces rather than retrospective narratives. Work in behavioral analytics and AI-assisted modeling therefore offers a useful path forward, particularly where resilience indicators can be calibrated against observable signatures in digital artifacts \autocite{rane2024artificial}.\\

Computational approaches, including agent-based modeling and simulation, are especially useful here. They allow researchers to explore how coordination patterns, incentive structures, and infrastructural configurations shape the capacity of organizations to absorb, reconfigure, and respond to disruption \autocite{gillard2023efficient,arnold2004informationsharing}. Used carefully, such methods do more than illustrate scenarios. They allow theory to be refined against simulated and empirical evidence and help bridge the gap between conceptual language and organization-level practice. In that sense, operationalization is not only about measurement. It is also about making resilience a capability that can be examined, compared, and deliberately improved.

\subsection{Resilience Across Organizational and Institutional Scopes}

A second research direction develops the scope dimension of the Enactment--Scope framework by asking where resilience mechanisms operate and how they are distributed across organizational, infrastructural, institutional, and inter-organizational settings.\\
\\
In digitally mediated environments, adaptive capacity is inherently distributed. It does not sit neatly inside a single team, technology, or governance layer. Governance architectures can enable resilience, but they can also constrain it by creating ambiguity in decision rights, accountability gaps, and escalation failures \autocite{ostrom1990governing,lebel2006governance}. These frictions may remain latent in stable periods, but they become visible under stress. They appear as delayed responses, constrained options, unclear ownership, and coordination breakdowns.\\

Studying these dynamics requires more than abstract references to governance. It calls for approaches that connect institutional theory, regulatory ethnography, and actor--network perspectives with empirical work on digital infrastructures and everyday organizational practice. The point is to understand how resilience is enabled or blocked in the lived realities of digitally coordinated work, not merely in formal schemes of control.\\

Figure \ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity} introduces an opportunity matrix that maps research directions for digital organizational resilience across four scopes: \emph{micro-level routines}, \emph{meso-level infrastructures}, \emph{macro-level institutions}, and \emph{interorganizational ecosystems}. This matrix helps identify where resilience mechanisms can be studied and where they may require institutional support. It also reveals a pattern that matters. Institutional friction and cooperation problems become especially salient at meso and macro levels, while integrative opportunities run across all four.\\

This matrix does not replace the enactment perspective. It complements it. The enactment dimension clarifies \emph{how} resilience becomes operationalized through indicators, design principles, and evaluative benchmarks. The scope dimension clarifies \emph{where} those mechanisms matter. Together, they provide the central logic of the Enactment--Scope framework by linking enacted mechanisms to the distributed locations in which adaptive capacity is organized.\\

A related issue concerns cooperation under pressure. Time-critical crises rarely respect organizational boundaries. Response depends on whether actors can coordinate quickly across firms, infrastructures, and institutional settings. Yet this is exactly where trust asymmetries, activation delays, and incentive misalignments often undermine performance. Theories of collective action, modular governance, and platform-mediated collaboration therefore provide an important starting point, especially in digital threat environments where rapid mobilization is essential \autocite{ostrom1990governing,brauer2008compartmental,mahajan2022participatory}.\\

Empirical work on modular security platforms such as MISP is especially relevant here because it shows how structural design can enable or inhibit timely collaboration. Participatory simulations and controlled experiments add another layer by identifying thresholds for cooperative engagement and the points at which collaboration begins to fail structurally \autocite{gillard2023efficient,skopik2016problem}. Disaster-response research offers additional design principles for cross-organizational information sharing through IT systems, and many of those insights are transferable to adversarial digital environments \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing}. Whether resilience becomes a substantive capability or remains largely symbolic depends in no small part on how these institutional arrangements and cooperation dynamics are configured across scopes.

\subsection{Temporal Dynamics of Resilience Enactment}

A third research direction extends the enactment perspective by asking how resilience is formed and re-formed over time. The point is not simply to add a temporal layer to the studies discussed above, but to examine how enacted capacities are built, stabilized, weakened, or redirected through experience, feedback, and institutional choice. On this view, resilience is neither a fixed organizational attribute nor something secured by resource availability alone. It is a contingent and relational capacity whose durability depends on whether earlier adaptations are reinforced, remembered, and carried forward, or instead neglected and allowed to erode \autocite{luthar2000construct,rutter1987psychosocial}. Yet this temporal dimension remains underdeveloped in much of the IS literature, which still tends to focus on discrete events or isolated responses rather than longer trajectories of adjustment. Organizations do not confront each disruption from a blank slate. They carry residues of earlier crises: routines, assumptions, temporary fixes, and interpretive habits that may support later adaptation, lock them into rigid responses, or simply fade over time. Any persuasive account of resilience therefore has to explain not only how organizations respond in the moment, but also how adaptive capacity is retained, revised, or lost across successive episodes of disruption.\\
\\
Useful foundations for this line of inquiry already exist in systems theory and ecology. Holling's work on adaptive cycles, for example, draws attention to recurring phases of growth, conservation, release, and reorganization, making clear that resilience is tied to sequences and transitions rather than to any single moment of adjustment \autocite{holling1973resilience}. Wiener's cybernetics places feedback at the center and shows how systems may be stabilized through corrective feedback or pushed toward instability through reinforcing feedback \autocite{wiener1948cybernetics}. Taken together, these traditions suggest that resilience depends not only on an organization's immediate ability to cope with disruption, but also on whether effective responses are reinforced, whether lessons become institutionalized, and whether failure produces revision rather than repetition. Under some conditions resilience accumulates over time; under others it decays through forgetting, inertia, or maladaptation.\\

Studying these dynamics calls for methodological pluralism. Longitudinal case studies are needed to follow how organizations adapt across repeated disruptions rather than single events. Process tracing can then identify the mechanisms through which learning practices, governance arrangements, and changes in technical systems shape later outcomes. Computational models offer complementary leverage by making it possible to examine how different rates of reinforcement, forgetting, or knowledge transfer generate different long-run resilience trajectories. Taken together, these approaches would allow IS research to move beyond largely static conceptions of resilience and instead analyze it as a socio-technical capability that can strengthen, deteriorate, or mutate as organizations are repeatedly disturbed.

\subsection{Toward an Integrative Enactment-Scope Research Agenda}

Taken together, these research directions point toward a broader agenda: resilience needs to be studied by linking mechanisms of enactment to the organizational scopes within which they operate. Fragmented perspectives will only take the field so far. What is needed instead is a socio-technical account that can relate behavioral processes, institutional conditions, and temporal development without collapsing them into a single level of analysis. This ambition is consistent with systems and ecological traditions that treat resilience as an emergent effect of feedback, interdependence, and adaptation \autocite{wiener1948cybernetics,holling1973resilience}. For IS research, that means building explanations that connect micro-level action, meso-level organizational and infrastructural dynamics, and macro-level governance constraints. It also means developing indicators that do more than register compliance. Useful indicators would need to capture adaptation as it unfolds: changes in response variability, shifts in coordination under stress, and the reconfiguration of technical and organizational arrangements during disruption \autocite{aerts2018integrating}. Unless resilience is anchored in observable behavior, it risks remaining rhetorically attractive without becoming analytically precise.\\
\\
At the same time, resilience does not emerge in a vacuum. The institutional and organizational environments in which actors operate shape what forms of adaptation are feasible in the first place. Rules, incentives, authority structures, and escalation pathways do not simply constrain action after disruption; they structure the range of possible responses before disruption occurs \autocite{ungar2021modeling,ostrom1990governing}. Existing field-based and computational studies already show that these conditions matter, often appearing as friction, delay, coordination breakdown, or reduced strategic flexibility. The harder task now is to turn these scattered insights into analytical frameworks that can travel across settings without losing explanatory force.\\

Figure \ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity} makes this research space visible by locating opportunities across micro behaviors, meso infrastructures, macro institutions, and inter-organizational ecosystems. Read together, Figures \ref{fig:triangle} and \ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity} define the central logic of the Enactment--Scope framework. The triangle specifies how resilience moves from rhetorical commitment to enacted practice. The opportunity matrix shows where those mechanisms can be studied and where intervention is most likely to matter. Advancing this agenda will require research programs that combine theoretical ambition with methodological range, including computational modeling, longitudinal case analysis, and field-based validation. Used together, these approaches offer a more credible path toward cumulative and transferable knowledge about organizational resilience \autocite{gillard2023efficient,kalisch2024neurobiology,teece2007explicating}. More importantly, they provide a stronger basis for IS theorizing about digital resilience by showing how adaptive capacity emerges through the interaction of technological architectures, organizational practices, and institutional environments. On this view, resilience is neither a slogan nor a checklist. It is an enacted, distributed, and revisable socio-technical capability for maintaining and reorganizing coordinated action under persistent uncertainty \autocite{weick2011managing,woods2015four}.

The gap between resilience as policy commitment and resilience as enacted capability persists because research seldom specifies where adaptive mechanisms operate or how they depend on one another across a socio-technical organization. The Enactment--Scope framework sets the stage for addressing this difficulty. It links three enactment types---technological, managerial, and collaborative---to three coordination scopes---infrastructural, organizational, and ecosystem---and to the translation mechanisms through which stated commitment becomes observable practice. That structure converts the implementation gap diagnosed in Section~\ref{sec:RG} into empirically grounded questions about enactment. Cyber-intensive settings make those questions especially salient: field interviews (Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}) describe organizations that already maintain business continuity plans, SOC operations, and DORA-aligned testing regimes---yet rarely connect that industry backbone to governance, workforce planning, or board evaluation.

The framework converts the preceding diagnosis into three empirical questions. \emph{Enactment}: through what technological, managerial, and collaborative mechanisms does stated resilience become observable behavior before and during disruption? \emph{Scope}: at which coordination boundaries---digital infrastructure, organizational unit, inter-firm ecosystem---do those mechanisms succeed or fail in combination? \emph{Time}: does enacted capacity survive successive incidents, enhance, or on the contrary, erode once compliance cycles close?

Two complementary streams follow for IS and management research. \emph{Theoretical synthesis} must reconcile---without collapsing---the partly incompatible traditions reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:Background}: engineering recovery and reliability \autocite{bruneau2003framework,panteli2017metrics}, ecological reorganization \autocite{holling1973resilience,folke2010resilience}, psychological and collective adaptation \autocite{luthar2000construct,ungar2021modeling}, institutional governance \autocite{ostrom1990governing}, and complex-systems emergence \autocite{gao2016universal,benbya2020complex,krakovska2023resilience}. Management and IS work has imported these lineages selectively; what remains is an integrative vocabulary---enactment mechanisms, coordination scopes, translation pathways---that specifies design and accountability without definitional drift \autocite{woods2015four,hillmann2021organizational}. \emph{Empirical transfer and validation} must move in a complementary direction: methods that already trace disruption, recovery, and cascade dynamics in engineering, public health, and complex systems \autocite{cutter2010disaster,dawson2011agentbased,francis2014metric,kruk2017building,aerts2018integrating} remain sparse in the management and cyber-risk cells of Table~\ref{tab:ResearchGaps}, where surveys and case studies still dominate. IS research is well placed to import and test them against digital organizational traces---SOC telemetry, recovery logs, exercise after-action reports, cross-firm sharing platforms---that organizations already generate but rarely analyze in order to connect to governance or board evaluation (Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}).

The subsections below follow three research streams---theory, measurement and modeling, and governance and design---each examined at the infrastructural, organizational, and ecosystem scopes of Figure~\ref{fig:enactment_scope}. Table~\ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity} adds a complementary prioritization lens. Its columns locate work across micro routines, meso infrastructures, macro institutions, and inter-organizational ecosystems; its rows distinguish six direction types---operational indicators, integrative frameworks, comparative and cumulative evidence, temporal and process markers, normative and value-based synthesis, and governance and incentives. Three patterns from the matrix structure the agenda below. First, \emph{operational indicators} and \emph{temporal/process markers} are high-opportunity wherever digital traces are dense---micro routines, meso infrastructures, and ecosystem platforms---but remain only promising at macro institutional scope until cross-jurisdictional reporting converges \autocite{bcbs2021operational,european2022regulation,nist2024csf}. Second, \emph{integrative frameworks} are most needed at meso infrastructures, macro institutions, and inter-organizational ecosystems, where partial literatures must be reconciled without collapsing into a single maturity score; at micro routines, validated indicators should precede further framework proliferation \autocite{hillmann2021organizational}. Third, \emph{governance and incentives} advances are promising where dependency maps and sharing platforms already exist---meso and ecosystem columns---but stall at macro scope without comparable benchmarks and at micro scope when traces remain confined to security operations \autocite{skopik2016problem}. \emph{Comparative and cumulative} work is similarly split: tractable across infrastructures and ecosystems, promising but harmonization-dependent at macro institutions, and low at micro routines until core indicators stabilize \autocite{francis2014metric}. Simulation and exploratory modeling address cells the matrix marks as under-specified---especially temporal dynamics at meso scope and path-dependent reorganization that single-firm traces cannot yet observe \autocite{dawson2011agentbased,kwakkel2016comparing}.

\subsection{Theory}

The opportunity matrix rates \emph{integrative frameworks} as high-opportunity at meso infrastructures, macro institutions, and inter-organizational ecosystems---precisely where Section~\ref{sec:Background}'s divergent traditions must be reconciled without definitional drift \autocite{woods2015four,hillmann2021organizational}. Before indicators or governance instruments can travel across settings, theory must settle what adaptive performance means, which mechanisms count as enactment, and how partly incompatible scientific traditions constrain design choices at each coordination scope. Three research directions follow---one per enactment--scope row of Figure~\ref{fig:enactment_scope}, aligned with the matrix columns above.

\textbf{Infrastructural scope (meso infrastructures).} Develop IS-informed theory of resilience when coordination is materially mediated by digital infrastructures. Build on generative entanglement between organizational routines and platform infrastructures \autocite{henfridsson2013generative}, coordination-design logic for unstable interdependence \autocite{galbraith1973designing}, and network-modularity arguments from cyber-resilience \autocite{albert2000error,linkov2013resilience,panteli2017metrics}. A concrete research direction is to specify when recovery of predefined functions, modular isolation, and reconfiguration under vendor or cloud dependency are competing success criteria---and which governance arrangements \autocite{boin2013resilient,dupont2019cyberresilience} make each criterion actionable. This directly addresses the matrix's high-opportunity \emph{integrative frameworks} cell at meso scope, where engineering recovery metrics and organizational governance logics still lack a shared operational vocabulary \autocite{bruneau2003framework,kuypers2018designing}.

\textbf{Organizational scope (micro routines).} The organizational-capability tradition already supplies most of what an integrative construct of \emph{digital organizational resilience} requires, but in pieces tuned to non-digital settings. High-reliability organizing and collective mindfulness explain how teams sustain reliable performance under hazard \autocite{vogus2007organizational,weick2011managing}, yet were theorized for tightly coupled physical operations rather than distributed, digitally mediated work. Dynamic-capability accounts specify how firms sense disruption and reconfigure resources \autocite{sambamurthy2003shaping,teece2007explicating,pavlou2010elusive,lengnick2011developing,duchek2019organizational}, but pitch that capacity at the level of strategy, leaving the micro-routines through which reconfiguration actually occurs---who coordinates with whom, how quickly, through which handoffs---largely unspecified. The structural--cultural enablers emphasized in resilience management---decentralization, redundancy, and an information culture that converts near-misses into learning---name the conditions that help \autocite{westrum2004typology,bhamra2011resilience,burnard2011organisational}, but treat them as standing properties rather than as behavior observable while it unfolds. The integrative task is to relocate these accounts into digitally mediated coordination, where the same routines leave traces, and to state the construct at the level at which those traces are generated. The matrix marks \emph{integrative frameworks} at micro routines as low-opportunity until core indicators exist; the concrete research task is therefore to specify observable boundary conditions under which bounded-rational coordination \autocite{march1993organizations,simon1997administrative} under live disruption differs from preparedness, audit posture, and generic adaptability, and to show how technological, managerial, and collaborative mechanisms combine inside the firm without collapsing into a single maturity score \autocite{hillmann2021organizational,hepfer2022heterogeneity,boh2023building}. Normative and value-based synthesis at this level should follow indicator validation rather than precede it \autocite{woods2015four}.

\textbf{Ecosystem scope (inter-organizational ecosystems).} Theorize collective resilience by linking institutional and IS accounts of cross-boundary coordination: polycentric and self-organizing governance \autocite{ostrom1990governing}, network-centric public-sector and platform arrangements \autocite{janssen2006network,liu2022network}, and information-sharing infrastructures for collective defense \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,lostri2022shared}. The concrete research task is to define emergent ecosystem resilience as dependence on cross-firm activation thresholds---when ISAC membership, shared standards, or platform interoperability translate into cooperative response under asymmetric threat, and when they remain declarative \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,bjorck2015cyber,enisa2017isacs,mermoud2019share}. This fills the matrix's high-opportunity \emph{integrative frameworks} and \emph{comparative and cumulative} cells at ecosystem scope, where collective defense infrastructures exist but lack cumulative theory of activated cooperation \autocite{skopik2016problem}.

\subsection{Measurement and Modeling}

If resilience is enacted rather than declared, it must be measured where it leaves traces. Table~\ref{fig:resilience_interface_opportunity} prioritizes \emph{operational indicators} and \emph{temporal/process markers} at micro routines, meso infrastructures, and ecosystem levels---matching the trace-rich settings digital organizations already populate \autocite{bruneau2003framework,francis2014metric,kuypers2018designing}. Digital organizations now produce continuous security and behavioral logs---SOC and Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) telemetry, ticket histories, access and recovery timelines, coordination metadata---at volumes that make adaptive performance potentially observable \autocite{howison2011validity,miloslavskaya2016security,kuypers2018designing}, provided those traces are interpreted as evidence of enactment rather than as compliance artifacts alone. Treating these traces as evidence rather than tickets aligns with the design-science tradition in IS \autocite{hevner2004design,gregor2013positioning,baskerville2015genres} and with recent calls to manage AI-augmented analytics as a research object in its own right \autocite{berente2021managing}. That opportunity invites a two-layer research strategy. A first layer applies quantitative, explainable methods from data science, computer science, and complex systems---chosen because they preserve visible assumptions that governance audiences can interrogate---and three are especially tractable against the traces organizations already hold. \emph{Event-sequence and survival modeling} turns incident and recovery logs into hazard curves for detection, response, and restoration: it estimates how quickly service is regained, how that distribution shifts across successive incidents---shrinking when learning compounds, lengthening when capacity erodes---and which conditions (team, dependency, time of day) raise the hazard of a prolonged outage, while properly handling incidents still unresolved at the point of measurement. \emph{Network inference} reconstructs the coordination network that actually carries a response---who hands off to whom across IT, risk, and compliance, and which services and vendors a recovery depends on---rather than the org chart; its structural signatures (brokers, redundant paths, structural holes, single points of failure) measure whether coordination degrades gracefully or fragments under load, and locate the silos field interviews flag \autocite{gao2016universal}. \emph{Perturbation--response analysis} treats drills and live incidents as shocks and reads the return trajectory---return time, overshoot, and early-warning signals such as rising variance and autocorrelation that mark critical slowing down before a regime shift \autocite{dawson2011agentbased,krakovska2023resilience}. Together these yield governance-facing signals---a measured recovery-time distribution to test against stated impact tolerances, a map of fragile handoffs to reinforce, and a leading indicator of declining resilience---each tied to a concrete action rather than an opaque risk score. A second layer integrates these foundations with machine-learning pipelines embedded in explainable artificial intelligence capable of rationalizing high-dimensional trace data without reducing adaptive performance to opaque risk scores \autocite{gillard2023efficient}.

Much of this analytical infrastructure already exists in commercial form, yet remains calibrated for detection rather than for evidencing adaptive capacity. SIEM, observability, and security-analytics platforms (e.g.\ Splunk, Elastic Security, Microsoft Sentinel) operate as ``data telescopes'' that surface incidents but rarely characterize resilience enactment; identity security posture platforms such as \href{https://saporo.io/}{Saporo} graph hybrid access dependencies and rank exploitable paths, generating operational indicators of posture and attack resistance but rarely consolidating them into governance rhythms or strategic decision cycles. The research task is therefore not only to import methods from adjacent fields, but to orient existing data-intelligence capabilities toward indicators that operators, boards, and regulators can interpret as evidence of adaptive performance. Three research directions follow, each targeting the matrix's highest-opportunity measurement cells at the corresponding scope.

\textbf{Infrastructural scope (meso infrastructures).} Import engineering and complex-systems methods---recovery-time objectives, network cascade models, perturbation--response and return-time dynamics---to measure how infrastructural interdependence shapes detection, reconfiguration, and service restoration during disruption \autocite{ives1995measuring,bruneau2003framework,gao2016universal,panteli2017metrics,krakovska2023resilience}. The matrix marks \emph{operational indicators} at meso scope as high-opportunity and \emph{temporal/process markers} as low-opportunity until longitudinal recovery traces accumulate at scale. Three research tasks follow: derive trace-based indicators of reconfiguration latency, cascade reach, and restoration drift relative to stated recovery objectives from dependency maps, failover logs, and hybrid topology metadata; calibrate cascade and perturbation--response models on those traces and test whether they predict return times observed in drills and live incidents \autocite{dawson2011agentbased,linkov2013resilience,ibnehossain2020modeling}; and evaluate whether recovery-orchestration platforms such as \href{https://www.resilio.io/}{Resilio}, which already productize hybrid-infrastructure measurement, can be recalibrated into governance-facing indicators of infrastructural enactment rather than tactical recovery metrics alone \autocite{francis2014metric,kuypers2018designing}.

\textbf{Organizational scope (micro routines).} Derive and validate micro-level indicators of adaptive performance---coordination latency across IT, risk, and compliance units; response variability under exercises; post-incident learning persistence; and internal coordination-network structure inferred from who routinely works with whom---from internal digital traces that field interviews show are collected but rarely linked to governance (Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}) \autocite{barasa2018what}. Both \emph{operational indicators} and \emph{temporal/process markers} are high-opportunity at micro scope---the densest trace environment in the matrix. Four research tasks follow: operationalize core indicators from event-sequence and handoff metadata in SOC and ticket systems, exercise logs, and post-incident documentation trails \autocite{kuypers2018designing,gillard2025quantitative}; infer internal coordination networks from collaboration traces that reveal durable ties of strength as well as fragile or overloaded handoffs; test construct validity by comparing trace-derived scores with exercise outcomes and successive incident response, and by distinguishing enacted adaptation from audit posture and generic preparedness \autocite{westrum2004typology,hepfer2022heterogeneity,boh2023building}; and evaluate whether coupling trace-based indicators to cross-functional governance rhythms---board reporting, workforce planning, and post-incident review---reduces the decision and learning gaps that interviews report when telemetry remains confined to security operations \autocite{francis2014metric}.

\textbf{Ecosystem scope (inter-organizational ecosystems).} Build comparative, cumulative metrics for cross-organizational coordination---information-sharing latency, joint-exercise performance, collective response activation---using platform logs, ISAC records, and sector-wide incident classifications now harmonized under DORA and Basel operational-resilience principles \autocite{ecb2018tiber,bcbs2021operational,european2022regulation}. The matrix marks \emph{operational indicators} at ecosystem scope as high-opportunity and \emph{comparative and cumulative} evidence as low-opportunity until shared baselines stabilize across participants \autocite{mermoud2019share,gillard2023efficient}. Commercial offerings such as \href{https://ccrc.nl/chain-cyber-crisis-exercises/}{CCRC Chain}, which convenes joint cyber-crisis simulations across supplier and partner boundaries in the Netherlands \autocite{ecb2018tiber,european2022regulation,ccrc2024chain}, sit more naturally at this scope: they stress-test how disruption propagates across third-party boundaries and, in effect, generate indicators of collective exposure \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience,hausken2020cyber}---yet rarely consolidate those findings into multi-party governance or sector-wide learning routines \autocite{skopik2016problem}. Two research tasks follow: operationalize and validate comparative indicators from platform logs, ISAC records, and harmonized incident classifications, testing whether they distinguish declarative membership from activated cooperation under asymmetric threat \autocite{enisa2017isacs,mermoud2019share}; and evaluate whether exercise and telemetry outputs feed multi-party governance rhythms---sector briefings, supplier escalation, shared after-action learning---rather than remaining confined to bilateral security workflows \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,skopik2016problem,lostri2022shared}.

Trace-based measurement nonetheless leaves important matrix cells under-specified. Conventional risk-management toolkits estimate exposure, loss, and control effectiveness under assumed threat models; they ask how severe outcomes might be and how probable they are. Resilience analysis asks a different question: under sustained or repeated disruption, does coordinated adaptive capacity stabilize, fragment, or reorganize---and along which pathways? That question is dynamic and path-dependent. It cannot be answered from audit snapshots or from correlational analysis on a single organizational history alone, and the limits of safety analysis in complex technological systems further constrain what such snapshots can reveal about pathways under reorganization \autocite{sornette2013exploring,woods2015four}. Simulation---especially agent-based and other generative models---therefore complements observational traces in the cells where the matrix marks \emph{temporal/process markers} or \emph{integrative frameworks} as low-opportunity despite their theoretical importance \autocite{macal2010tutorial,dawson2011agentbased,nan2011capturing,helbing2012agent,aerts2018integrating}. Where digital logs reveal what did happen inside one firm or platform, simulation explores counterfactual ensembles of how technological, managerial, and collaborative mechanisms interact across infrastructural, organizational, and ecosystem boundaries before live disruption \autocite{gao2016universal,krakovska2023resilience}. Under deep uncertainty about adversarial adaptation, vendor coupling, and cross-unit coordination, the aim is not point prediction but \emph{scenario discovery}: identifying recurrent outcome regimes, the structural levers that sort configurations among them, and the observation horizons at which diverging pathways become distinguishable \autocite{haasnoot2013dynamic,kwakkel2016comparing,lempert2019robust}. Generative work on adversarial cooperation dynamics illustrates why this matters for resilience beyond risk scoring: endpoint cooperation can mask qualitatively different trajectories---ongoing reorganization versus stabilized containment---and the futures hardest to anticipate from partial evidence are often those heading toward systemic degradation rather than toward quiet recovery \autocite{walker2004resilience}. Monitoring design must therefore pair defender-side traces with threat-side proxies and concentrate on medium-horizon windows when pathways are actively separating, rather than assume that more data or longer observation automatically improves foresight \autocite{francis2014metric}.

Four simulation-linked research tasks follow, each targeting an under-specified matrix intersection. First, embed enactment--scope mechanisms in generative models that couple infrastructure cascades, internal coordination latencies, and cross-firm sharing rules---addressing low-opportunity \emph{temporal/process markers} at meso scope---and map where combined failures produce qualitatively different recovery trajectories rather than scalar risk scores \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,dawson2011agentbased,linkov2013resilience}. Second, use exploratory modeling and stress testing to identify high-leverage structural levers---modularity, escalation design, sharing activation thresholds---that partition the outcome space under uncertainty \autocite{cutter2010disaster}. Third, calibrate simulation outputs against the trace-based indicators developed above and against exercise data (including chain-wide simulations such as CCRC Chain), treating models as governance-facing scenario laboratories rather than opaque forecast engines \autocite{ecb2018tiber,ccrc2024chain}. Fourth, identify observation horizons and monitoring signposts at which diverging resilience pathways become distinguishable before outcomes stabilize---building the temporal evidence base the matrix currently lacks at meso and macro scope \autocite{francis2014metric,kwakkel2016comparing,meerow2016defining,nist2024csf}.

\subsection{Governance and Design}

Governance and design are usually cast as the stable scaffolding that translates observed adaptive performance into decision rights, escalation structure, and aligned incentives \autocite{boin2013resilient,calder2021iso,bcbs2021operational,nist2024csf}. We take a stronger position: resilience is itself a moment in which governance \emph{changes}. When disruption departs from script, authority migrates---decision rights shift toward those closest to the problem, escalation paths are short-circuited, standing committees give way to incident command, and improvised coordination temporarily displaces routine control \autocite{weick2011managing,boin2013resilient}. This transient governance is where adaptive performance is actually produced, yet it is seldom studied as a governed object in its own right. Four questions follow. How does the shift \emph{appear}---what tips an organization out of routine governance, and through which signals is the transition recognized? Is it \emph{anticipated}---pre-authorized and rehearsed---or improvised each time under legal, audit, and political risk? Is it \emph{reintegrated}---do the decisions, deviations, and lessons of the disrupted period flow back into the standing governance system, or are they discarded once the crisis closes and the org chart reasserts itself? And what does this alternative governance \emph{need to work}: which pre-granted decision rights, information access, trust relationships, and explicit handback protocols let authority migrate fast and return cleanly, without either paralysis or unaccountable improvisation? Treating these transitions as designable---rather than merely tolerated---is the core of a governance agenda for resilience. Against that backdrop, the opportunity matrix locates \emph{governance and incentives} as promising at meso infrastructures and inter-organizational ecosystems, but low at macro institutions until comparable benchmarks exist and at micro routines until indicators connect traces to decision rights \autocite{skopik2016problem,bcbs2021operational,nist2024csf}. Three research directions follow, each aligned with the matrix's most tractable governance cells at the corresponding enactment--scope level.

\textbf{Infrastructural scope (meso infrastructures).} Specify design principles for infrastructure governance under stress---access control, vendor escalation, operator--provider coordination, governance of critical software updates, service-restoration priorities---and evaluate them against benchmarks derived from recovery logs and third-party dependency maps \autocite{ecb2018tiber,hausken2020cyber}. The matrix marks this cell as promising rather than fully tractable: governance instruments exist, but benchmark harmonization across providers and jurisdictions remains incomplete \autocite{bcbs2021operational,european2022regulation}. The research task is to validate those principles jointly with the trace-based indicators developed above \autocite{kuypers2018designing}.

\textbf{Organizational scope (micro routines).} Design governance arrangements that couple siloed units---IT, risk, compliance, operations---through shared indicators, rehearsal routines, and explicit decision protocols---including the pre-authorized rules by which decision authority migrates to the point of disruption when an incident is declared and is handed back, with an audit trail, once it closes---rather than folding resilience into existing committees without cross-functional mandate (Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}) \autocite{weick2011managing,bhamra2011resilience}. The matrix rates \emph{governance and incentives} at micro scope as low-opportunity until the operational indicators above reach board-facing validity; research should therefore test governance designs jointly with trace-based measurement rather than as standalone policy templates. These arrangements should be evaluated against the trace-based indicators developed above and stress-tested through simulation-derived scenario laboratories \autocite{weick2011managing,bhamra2011resilience}.

\textbf{Ecosystem scope (inter-organizational ecosystems).} Develop warning systems and incentive structures that sustain cross-firm cooperation when activation delays, trust asymmetries, and platform-design frictions would otherwise block collective response---drawing on modular security platforms, bug-bounty governance, disaster-response information-sharing design, and behavioral accounts of why ISAC participation often underutilizes sharing despite its collective benefits \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,maillart2017bugbounty,mermoud2019share,lostri2022shared}. This addresses the matrix's promising \emph{governance and incentives} cell at ecosystem scope, where sharing infrastructures exist but activation remains fragile \autocite{mermoud2019share,gillard2023efficient}. These structures should be evaluated against trace-based indicators, exercise outputs, and simulation-derived scenario findings \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,maillart2017bugbounty,mermoud2019share,lostri2022shared}.

\subsection{An Integrative Development Path}

The directions above imply a corresponding sequence of \emph{scientific contributions}, ordered so that each class of study supplies what the next must build on; without that order the literature accrues publications but not cumulative knowledge. The arc opens with \emph{conceptual and measurement-development} work: contributions that fix enacted adaptive performance as a construct and propose trace-based indicators with explicit operational definitions, so that independent studies measure the same thing \autocite{francis2014metric,hillmann2021organizational}. These enable a second class of \emph{validation and calibration studies}, which establish that the indicators track adaptive performance against exercise outcomes and successive incidents and that simulation models reproduce observed recovery dynamics rather than merely illustrate them \autocite{dawson2011agentbased,kwakkel2016comparing}. Shared, validated instruments are the precondition for \emph{integrative and comparative theory}: contributions that carry indicators across the infrastructural and ecosystem scopes and reconcile the partly incompatible traditions of Section~\ref{sec:Background} without collapsing them into a single maturity score \autocite{woods2015four,hillmann2021organizational}. A fourth class is \emph{design-science and intervention research}---artifacts and field studies that bind validated indicators to decision rights, escalation, and the transient-governance transitions above, and test whether instrumented governance measurably improves adaptation \autocite{boin2013resilient,bcbs2021operational}. The arc closes with \emph{cumulative and replication studies}: multi-organization, multi-sector, and longitudinal designs, shared trace datasets, and the benchmarks they support, which turn isolated findings into evidence that travels and can anchor cross-jurisdictional comparison at the macro scope \autocite{european2022regulation,gillard2023efficient}. This is the desired trajectory of contributions for IS research---construct, then instrument, then theory, then intervention, then cumulative comparison---each a recognizable type of study and each a precondition for the credibility of the next.
