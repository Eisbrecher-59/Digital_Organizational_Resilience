\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:Introduction}
\renewcommand{\baselinestretch}{2.0}\normalsize

In early 2023, engineers at Samsung inadvertently exposed proprietary source code by entering confidential information into publicly accessible generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools, prompting the company to ban the use of ChatGPT and similar systems among employees to prevent further leaks \autocite{ray2023samsung}. This incident was not an isolated anomaly. Since then, large organizations have reported a growing number of generative-AI-related data exposures and security violations \autocite{arora2025understanding}. More broadly, advanced AI systems are expected to automate and accelerate cyberattacks, lowering the entry barrier for sophisticated threats and enabling malicious actors to scale operations in ways previously constrained by human and technical resources \autocite{arif2024overview}.\\

These developments reflect a broader shift in the conditions under which organizations operate. As digital technologies have become more deeply embedded in organizational processes, coordination, and interdependence, disruptions increasingly arise not only from technical failures, but also from human practices and their interaction with technical systems \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}. Such disruptions cannot be addressed through technical safeguards or formal policies alone. They require organizations to build capabilities for adaptation and coordination across intertwined human and technical components under conditions of uncertainty \autocite{boin2013resilient}. Resilience therefore concerns the organizational ability to adjust, reorganize, and sustain operations as disturbances unfold \autocite{burnard2011organisational}.\\

Against this backdrop, resilience has become a central regulatory priority \autocite{heinimann2017infrastructure}. Crises such as COVID-19 \autocite{almeida2020challenges}, large-scale cyber incidents \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}, and systemic information technology failures \autocite{pumpuni2017resilience} have moved resilience from an aspirational organizational goal toward a governance commitment \autocite{boin2013resilient}. Across sectors ranging from finance to healthcare and critical infrastructure, organizations are now expected to anticipate, absorb, and adapt to disruption while maintaining operational continuity and legitimacy. This expectation is reinforced by landmark regulatory initiatives such as the European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and Australia's Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018, which formalize resilience requirements in sectors exposed to digital and cyber disruption \autocite{SOCI2018,european2022regulation}. These initiatives represent a significant collective effort by regulators, standard-setting bodies, and organizations to make resilience an explicit object of governance. At the same time, practitioners report that organizations often find it difficult to adapt at the pace of an accelerating and unstable technological environment \autocite{paries2017resilience}. A persistent gap therefore remains between regulatory ambition and organizational capability: managers continue to grapple with what resilience means for their specific context, and with how to measure and operationalize it \autocite{bjorck2015cyber}. Organizations also increasingly face disruptions that are continuous, novel, and overlapping, whereas many existing assurance systems were originally designed for a limited set of exceptional shocks. As a result, audit-driven compliance and control frameworks—important as they are for accountability—tend to emphasize documentation and formal assurance, and provide a more limited window onto the learning, coordination, and flexible response that adaptive capacity also requires \autocite{power1999audit, woods2015four}.\\

These tensions are not confined to the literature; they are also visible in organizational practice. To complement the review, we draw on an exploratory qualitative study based on 18 semi-structured interviews with cybersecurity and risk professionals from large organizations in Switzerland, selected for their involvement in cyber risk management and organizational resilience issues (see Appendix \ref{appendix:C} for the full study design, sample characteristics, interview protocol, and detailed results). The interviews highlight the genuine effort organizations devote to resilience and surface six recurring themes (see Table \ref{tab:RotaResult}): (i) the difficulty of defining and quantifying resilience, (ii) the centrality of regulatory expectations and the corresponding emphasis on demonstrable compliance \autocite{power1999audit}, (iii) coordination challenges across organizational silos, (iv) the limited extent to which incidents are converted into opportunities for organizational learning, (v) the uneven integration of human and cultural factors, and (vi) the tension between regulatory demands for flexibility and organizational demands for precision. Taken together, these observations reinforce a central point of this review: resilience is widely prescribed and seriously pursued, but the means of enacting it as an organizational capability are still maturing.\\

These practical difficulties are accompanied by a notable plurality of meaning in the scientific literature. As resilience has developed across disciplines and industries, its referents have multiplied, spanning system uptime, business continuity, regulatory posture, and security architecture \autocite{woods2015four,hillmann2021organizational}. Each of these usages reflects a substantive contribution to the field, but the variety also creates challenges for conceptual coherence and practical accountability. Even where organizations adopt established resilience and risk-management practices -- such as audits, business continuity planning, incident response procedures, or post-incident reviews -- the conversion of incidents and near-misses into sustained organizational learning still depends on supporting routines for reflexivity, decentralization, and cross-unit coordination that are themselves under active development \autocite{francis2014metric,barasa2018what}. A growing body of standards and guidelines now provides important reference points for resilience \autocite{linkov2019fundamental}, and the next step is to complement them with methods that quantify, simulate, design, and implement resilience in ways that organizations can routinely apply \autocite{kuypers2018designing}. These efforts are made more demanding by the fact that resilience has been translated into organizational settings through partially divergent assumptions about stability, adaptation, control, and transformation.\\
\\
Advancing resilience in organizational settings therefore requires a clearer understanding of resilience as a socio-technical capability. To address this challenge, we adopt an integrative and problem-driven approach. Rather than adding another abstract definition or prescribing generic best practices, we examine why translation from concept to organizational capability is difficult, and where the work of regulators, practitioners, and researchers can most usefully be extended. By tracing how resilience has been conceptualized, operationalized, and enacted across disciplines -- and where these translations encounter friction -- we shift the analytical focus from what resilience ought to mean to how it can be made observable and actionable in organizational settings.\\
