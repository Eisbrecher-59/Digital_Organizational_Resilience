\section{\MakeUppercase {The Enactment-Scope Framework}}
\label{sec:Enactment-Scope}
\renewcommand{\baselinestretch}{2.0}\normalsize


The preceding analysis points to a productive question for resilience research. Resilience is widely embraced as a desirable organizational capability, and many strands of the literature have made significant progress on partial elements of it—infrastructure robustness, organizational preparedness, or inter-organizational coordination—even as the integration of these elements into adaptive performance in digitally mediated environments remains an open task \autocite{burnard2011organisational,boin2013resilient}. The challenge is therefore one of synthesis: to show how these contributions combine into operational, comparable, and evaluable forms across organizational settings \autocite{linkov2013resilience,ibnehossain2020modeling}.\\

A constructive next step is to specify the methods by which resilience becomes an observable and governable organizational capability. Figure~\ref{fig:triangle} illustrates this move from articulated commitment to enacted practice. Building on that logic, the Enactment-Scope framework specifies where resilience mechanisms operate and how they are realized across digitally mediated organizational systems. Operational indicators make adaptive mechanisms visible. Design principles define decision rights and structure coordination across organizational units. Benchmarks and warning systems align incentives and signal movement away from desired states. Together, these mechanisms help translate resilience from a stated organizational commitment into enacted capability in practice \autocite{linkov2013resilience,boin2013resilient,ibnehossain2020modeling}.\\

That still leaves a question: where exactly do these mechanisms operate in digitally mediated organizational systems? Contemporary organizations function within socio-technical environments composed of digital infrastructures, organizational processes, and inter-organizational ecosystems \autocite{liu2022network,gillard2023efficient}. Resilience mechanisms operate across different socio-technical arrangements and scopes of coordination. The Enactment-Scope framework is crafted to seize that structure by organizing resilience mechanisms according to how they are enacted and the scopes within which they operate.

\subsection{Enactment of Resilience}

The first dimension of the framework concerns the \emph{enactment} of resilience and distinguishes three types of mechanism through which adaptive capacity is actually produced. Resilience is not treated as an abstract attribute that organizations simply possess. It emerges through concrete mechanisms embedded in technological systems, managerial practices, and collaborative arrangements \autocite{burnard2011organisational,weick2011managing,duchek2019organizational}. \emph{Technological} mechanisms include redundancy, monitoring, and automated recovery built into digital infrastructure \autocite{linkov2013resilience,bjorck2015cyber,kuypers2018designing}. \emph{Managerial} mechanisms include incident-response routines, learning practices, and decision protocols that allocate authority during disruption \autocite{vogus2007organizational,weick2011managing,duchek2019organizational}. \emph{Collaborative} mechanisms include information sharing, joint planning, and coordinated response across organizational boundaries \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,enisa2017isacs,dupont2019cyberresilience,gillard2023efficient}.\\
\\
Viewing resilience through enactment shifts attention from abstract claims to concrete socio-technical activity. Adaptive capacity arises from interactions among human actors, digital infrastructures, and institutional arrangements \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience,liu2022network}. In digitally mediated environments, resilient performance depends on whether mechanisms are implemented, activated under disruption, and enable organizations to detect, respond to, and adapt as conditions evolve \autocite{linkov2013resilience,duchek2019organizational}. This makes enactment analytically important as it shifts focus from theoretical claims to practical implementations, thereby providing a clearer understanding of resilience in action.

\subsection{Scope of Resilience}

The second dimension concerns the \emph{scope} at which resilience mechanisms operate. Contemporary organizations rarely function in isolation, embedded in digitally interconnected environments composed of infrastructures, platforms, and collaborative networks \autocite{liu2022network,gillard2023efficient}. Resilience mechanisms may operate across multiple scopes of coordination rather than within a single organizational boundary.\\
\\
At the \emph{organizational} scope, resilience mechanisms are primarily internal to the firm, including governance structures, risk-management practices, and technological safeguards intended to maintain continuity of operations \autocite{burnard2011organisational,duchek2019organizational}. At the \emph{infrastructural} scope, resilience depends on the robustness and recoverability of shared digital systems like communication networks, cloud platforms, and critical information infrastructures \autocite{linkov2013resilience,ibnehossain2020modeling}. At the \emph{ecosystem} scope, resilience emerges through coordinated action across organizational boundaries, including information sharing, collaborative incident response, and collective governance arrangements \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience,gillard2023efficient}.\\

Distinguishing these scopes clarifies that resilience in digital environments often depends on interactions among mechanisms operating across several layers of coordination \autocite{boin2013resilient,ibnehossain2020modeling}. An organization's response to a cyber incident may depend not only on its internal detection and response capabilities, but also on the resilience of external infrastructures and the responsiveness of partner organizations across the wider ecosystem \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience,gillard2023efficient}. Our field interviews (Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}, theme iii) confirm the practical relevance of this scope distinction: even within the organizational column of the framework, IT, risk, and compliance units are often described as operating in functional silos that constrain how internal mechanisms can be combined, let alone coupled to infrastructural and ecosystem-level coordination (\textit{``We only speak with risk after something happens---not before''}). Scope matters because the location of a resilience mechanism conditions both what it can achieve and what it depends on, highlighting the need for understanding interactions across different scopes.

\subsection{Integrating Enactment and Scope}

Bringing enactment and scope together provides a lens for examining how resilience mechanisms interact across socio-technical configurations. The framework shows how adaptive capacity emerges through the interaction of technological infrastructures, organizational practices, and ecosystem-level coordination mechanisms \autocite{boin2013resilient,duchek2019organizational,liu2022network}. It makes possible analysis of how resilience mechanisms reinforce one another, and where they generate dependencies, bottlenecks, blind spots, or misalignments across coordination scopes within digitally mediated organizational systems.\\

This is also where the framework departs from more generic multi-level accounts. It does not simply note that resilience involves several layers. It clarifies why resilience may appear strong at one scope while remaining fragile at another. An organization may possess well-developed internal response routines but depend on brittle infrastructures or unresponsive ecosystem partners. Conversely, robust infrastructures may not produce resilience when organizational routines are poorly aligned or cross-boundary coordination fails. The framework makes visible not only the distribution of resilience mechanisms but also the interdependencies through which resilient or non-resilient outcomes are produced.\\

The framework also helps explain why resilience is often difficult to operationalize in organizational contexts, primarily due to the complexity of integrating mechanisms across socio-technical layers. Many resilience initiatives focus on mechanisms at only one scope---internal preparedness, technological redundancy, or external coordination---and the analytical task of how these mechanisms interact across layers has been less developed \autocite{burnard2011organisational,linkov2013resilience,boin2013resilient,allen2018quantifying}. By linking mechanisms of enactment to the scopes within which they operate, the Enactment--Scope framework offers a more structured way to analyze how resilience arises from the interplay of technological, managerial, and collaborative processes across organizational, infrastructural, and ecosystem scopes.\\

Figure~\ref{fig:enactment_scope} renders the framework as a 3$\times$3 grid that locates concrete resilience mechanisms at their socio-technical address. Reading rows top to bottom, \emph{technological} mechanisms operate through digital safeguards: monitoring, redundancy, and automated recovery at the organizational scope \autocite{linkov2013resilience,bjorck2015cyber,kuypers2018designing}; cloud resilience, network modularity, and platform recoverability at the infrastructural scope \autocite{albert2000error,gao2016universal,panteli2017metrics,ibnehossain2020modeling}; and shared infrastructure, interoperability, and distributed defense at the ecosystem scope \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience,lostri2022shared,gillard2023efficient}. \emph{Managerial} mechanisms operate through human decision processes: incident response, learning routines, and decision protocols inside the firm \autocite{vogus2007organizational,weick2011managing,duchek2019organizational,boh2023building}; infrastructure governance, access control, and escalation design at the infrastructural scope \autocite{boin2013resilient,calder2021iso,finma2023operational,nist2024csf}; and cross-boundary governance, joint planning, and shared standards at the ecosystem scope \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,baselcommittee2011basel,bcbs2021operational,european2022regulation}. \emph{Collaborative} mechanisms operate through coordinated action: internal cross-functional response and knowledge sharing inside the firm \autocite{lengnick2011developing,bhamra2011resilience,henfridsson2013generative}; operator--provider coordination, vendor response, and service restoration at the infrastructural scope \autocite{skopik2016problem,ecb2018tiber,hausken2020cyber}; and information sharing, collective response, and ecosystem adaptation across organizational boundaries \autocite{janssen2006network,enisa2017isacs,liu2022network,mahajan2022participatory}. Each cell names mechanisms that are well discussed within their own row or column. What is comparatively underdeveloped is the analysis of how the nine cells interact when an organization is actually disturbed---which is precisely where the framework directs attention.\\

Figure~\ref{fig:enactment_scope} also resonates with Figure~\ref{fig:triangle}. The triangle identifies three translation mechanisms---operational indicators, design principles, and benchmarks \& warnings---through which articulated resilience becomes enacted capability. The matrix locates those mechanisms in concrete socio-technical positions. Operational indicators apply most directly to the technological row, where they render redundancy, recoverability, and reconfiguration empirically visible \autocite{bruneau2003framework,francis2014metric,kuypers2018designing,gillard2023efficient}. Design principles apply most directly to the managerial row, where they allocate decision rights, codify escalation, and structure governance \autocite{boin2013resilient,calder2021iso,bcbs2021operational,nist2024csf}. Benchmarks and warnings apply most directly to the collaborative row, where they align expectations and produce signals that travel across organizational boundaries \autocite{enisa2017isacs,ecb2018tiber,linkov2019fundamental,european2022regulation}. Read together, the two figures specify both \emph{what} translation mechanisms are needed (Figure~\ref{fig:triangle}) and \emph{where} they have to land in the socio-technical arrangement of digital organizations (Figure~\ref{fig:enactment_scope}).\\

In that sense, the framework advances resilience research in two ways. First, it shows how adaptive capacity emerges from the interaction of technological infrastructures, managerial practices, and ecosystem-level coordination mechanisms \autocite{weick2011managing,boin2013resilient,duchek2019organizational,liu2022network}. Second, it provides an analytical basis for developing operational measures that capture how resilience mechanisms function across digitally mediated organizational environments, thereby supporting empirical assessment, simulation-based analysis, and governance-oriented evaluation of digital resilience \autocite{linkov2013resilience,francis2014metric,ibnehossain2020modeling,krakovska2023resilience}. Beyond resilience as recovery or adaptation, the framework also accommodates more demanding regimes in which organizations \emph{gain} structurally from exposure to disorder---the antifragile case in Taleb's sense \autocite{taleb2012antifragile}---by treating learning routines, reconfiguration capacity, and ecosystem-level information sharing as enacted, observable mechanisms rather than aspirational properties. By moving from broad organizational commitments to specifications, the framework makes it possible to identify where adaptive capacity is located, how it is enacted, and what conditions support or constrain it under disruption.
