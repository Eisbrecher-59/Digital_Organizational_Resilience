\section{Implications for Research & Practice}
\label{sec:ImplicationsResearch}

Addressing the gaps identified in this review is not only a matter of filling omissions in the literature -- it requires a reorientation of how resilience is theorized, measured, and enacted. The following five implications articulate how future IS research can move resilience from a rhetorical aspiration to a tractable socio-technical capability. Together, they signal a shift from descriptive accounts toward actionable, model-based insights with both theoretical rigor and managerial relevance.\\

The first implication is ontological. Resilience must be clarified as an emergent sociotechnical capability rather than a static attribute. Conceptual ambiguity has long hindered cumulative theorization, with resilience often conflated with robustness or agility \autocite{woods2015four,duchek2020organizational}. By modeling organizations as adaptive systems, IS scholarship can anchor resilience in theories of dynamic capabilities and digital infrastructures \autocite{henfridsson2013generative,boh2023building}. This perspective resonates with ecological and systems theory traditions \autocite{holling1973resilience,wiener1948cybernetics}, but it adds the crucial insight that resilience emerges through the interplay of human, technological, and institutional elements in digital environments. Clarifying resilience ontologically is a prerequisite for subsequent advances.\\

Second, resilience must be rendered observable. Developing behavioral and computational indicators transforms resilience from metaphor into measurement. Promising markers include coordination speed, community ties, stakeholder support \cite{bruno roche}, infrastructural reconfiguration capacity, and variability of responses under stress \autocite{hollnagel2013resilience,gillard2023efficient}. Computational modeling—agent-based simulations, network analysis, and probabilistic models—can test how micro-level interactions scale into system-level outcomes \autocite{teece2007explicating}. Such approaches enable researchers to calibrate resilience against empirical traces, bridging conceptual clarity with practical application. This line of work directly responds to the criticism that resilience is “invoked often, enacted rarely” by providing organization-ready tools for both scholars and managers.\\

Third, resilience research must be empirically grounded. Too often, resilience has been assessed through compliance artifacts -- audits, certifications, maturity models -- rather than through the analysis of behavior under disruption \autocite{skopik2016problem}. Future work must engage deeply with field contexts through longitudinal case studies, trace data, and ethnographic inquiry. Healthcare and public health research has already shown how resilience is observed in practice yet rarely institutionalized \autocite{kruk2017building,barasa2018what}. Similar opportunities exist in digital infrastructures, where incidents and near-misses can reveal how organizations adapt -- or fail to adapt -- over time. Empirical grounding ensures that resilience is theorized not only as a desirable principle but as an enacted capability, tethered to the realities of organizational management.\\

Fourth, integration across levels and methods is essential. Resilience unfolds simultaneously at the level of micro routines, meso infrastructures, macro institutions, and inter-organizational ecosystems. Studying one level in isolation risks partial accounts that cannot explain systemic outcomes. IS research is particularly well positioned to bridge these levels, drawing on traditions of multi-level theorizing and methodological pluralism. Programs that combine process tracing, computational modeling, and comparative case analysis can connect individual behaviors to institutional constraints and ecosystem-level dynamics \autocite{ostrom1990governing,vogus2007organizational,folke2010resilience}. Such integration enables cumulative theorization and the development of standardized yet context-sensitive metrics that can travel across domains.\\

Finally, resilience research must sharpen its managerial relevance by articulating actionable design principles. Conceptual clarity and indicators will remain of limited value if they do not translate into tools that managers can deploy. Benchmarks, early-warning systems, and governance mechanisms should be designed to align incentives, support collaboration, and prevent ``metrics theater,'' where resilience is signaled but not practiced \autocite{weick2011managing,lengnick2011developing,calder2021iso}. IS scholarship can contribute here by producing frameworks that link measurement to governance, ensuring that resilience is cultivated as a capability rather than displayed as a compliance artifact.\\

Taken together, these five implications point to a significant reorientation of resilience research in IS. Rather than descriptive or compliance-driven accounts, the field can deliver actionable, model-based insights into how organizations anticipate, adapt, and evolve. For scholars, this opens opportunities to develop integrative theories and cumulative evidence. For managers, it means moving beyond slogans toward measurable and testable capabilities. Recasting resilience in this way can help IS scholarship move from symbolic rhetoric to a grounded capability for navigating complexity in digital environments.
