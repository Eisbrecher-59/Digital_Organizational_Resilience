\section{\MakeUppercase {Background}}
\label{sec:Background}
\renewcommand{\baselinestretch}{2.0}\normalsize

Before resilience can be enacted as an organizational capability, its scientific lineage must be understood. The term is now ubiquitous in policy and practice, but it entered management and information systems research through partially divergent intellectual traditions---each carrying distinct assumptions about whether systems should rebound to equilibrium, persist through reorganization, adapt under stress, or sustain collective action under uncertainty. Ignoring those roots makes resilience appear more coherent than it is and obscures why organizations can invoke the same label while pursuing different metrics, interventions, and governance logics \autocite{hillmann2021organizational,woods2015four,sarker2019sociotechnical}. Reviewing this genealogy is therefore not a historical detour but a methodological prerequisite: each scientific tradition fixes what counts as a disturbance, what should persist, and how recovery or adaptation is to be measured, so the constructs and metrics available to organizational research are inherited---knowingly or not---from these origins. Tracing them is what makes the field's persistent difficulty in defining, comparing, and operationalizing resilience legible rather than puzzling, and it is the only way to determine which mechanisms and measures each tradition makes analytically available for the integrative synthesis developed later in the paper. We therefore examine that genealogy across disciplines and its subsequent translation into management and information systems (IS) research.

\subsection{How Resilience Was Shaped Across Disciplines}

The paragraphs below proceed from equilibrium-oriented foundations, through the ecological challenge to restoration, into human and institutional adaptation, and finally to integrative socio-technical accounts.

\paragraph{Mathematics, Physics, and Engineering}
The earliest foundations of resilience emerged in mathematics and physics, where it was generally understood as the capacity of a system to absorb disturbance and return to equilibrium. Early formulations emphasized elasticity, stability, and homeostatic feedback as defining features of resilient behavior \autocite{rankine1858manual,wiener1948cybernetics}. This perspective proved especially useful for formal and quantitative treatments of engineered systems because disturbance-response dynamics can be specified with considerable precision. Control theory and engineering extended the same logic into applied domains such as infrastructure, safety, and risk management, defining resilience as recovery of functionality after disruption and measuring it through recovery time, reliability, and functional uptime \autocite{hashimoto1982reliability,bruneau2003framework}. In resilience engineering, \textcite{paries2017resilience} identified four practical capabilities---responding, monitoring, learning, and anticipating---as key levers for improving system resilience (see Appendix \ref{appendix:A1}). This equilibrium-oriented tradition offers strong operational clarity, but leaves relatively little room for cases in which systems do not return to a prior stable state, and its limits become more visible in adversarial, non-stationary settings such as cybersecurity \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}.

\paragraph{Biology and Ecology}
Biology and ecology changed the meaning of resilience more fundamentally. The emphasis moved from equilibrium restoration toward persistence through variability, adaptation, and regime change. Resilience no longer meant return to a prior state so much as the capacity to reorganize while preserving core functions \autocite{holling1973resilience,gunderson2002panarchy}. This perspective stresses that resilience emerges through feedbacks across ecological, social, and institutional levels \autocite{sundstrom2014transdisciplinary}. Ecological models such as the adaptive cycle illustrate transitions between stability regimes and show how diversity and redundancy can buffer systems against collapse \autocite{folke2010resilience}. Early metrics such as return variance, autocorrelation, and recovery time were used to characterize resilience under uncertainty \autocite{ives1995measuring} (see Appendix \ref{appendix:A2}). This tradition treats resilience as persistence through reorganization rather than simple recovery, but once transformation enters the picture, the relevant baseline, success criteria, and performance measures become harder to specify.

\paragraph{Engineering, Ecology, and Antifragility}
These two lineages rest on different assumptions. Engineering resilience emphasizes recovery to a known state, while ecological resilience accepts irreversibility and the possibility that persistence requires transformation \autocite{holling1996engineering,walker2004resilience}. A further extension, articulated by \textcite{taleb2012antifragile}, treats some systems as antifragile: capable of gaining structurally from variability and stress. In organizational settings this spectrum highlights mechanisms---deliberate exposure to controlled stress, redundancy designed for option value, compounding learning loops---that can strengthen adaptive capacity over time. That range of possibilities becomes difficult to ignore where resilience is invoked as if stability, adaptation, transformation, and antifragility formed a single coherent objective \autocite{woods2015four}.

\paragraph{Neuroscience and Psychology}
Extending the spectrum of adaptive responses from systems to the actors within them, research in neuroscience and psychology frames resilience as the capacity of individuals and groups to endure, adapt, and grow through adversity \autocite{luthar2000construct}. This work highlights mechanisms such as cognitive flexibility, emotional regulation, and experience-dependent plasticity \autocite{feder2019biology}. Psychometric instruments operationalize these capacities at the individual and small-group level \autocite{wagnild1993development,campbell2007psychometric}, while multisystemic perspectives locate them within broader personal, social, and institutional contexts \autocite{ungar2021modeling}. In high-stakes organizational domains such as cybersecurity operations and emergency response, these insights illuminate how stress, judgment, and coordination shape adaptive action under pressure \autocite{kruk2017building}. What remains underdeveloped, however, is the link to socio-technical organization research: while individual resilience has been extensively measured, far less is known about how these capacities scale to collective organizational processes or interact with technological infrastructures---the very translation problem that the present review seeks to address.

\paragraph{Institutional Economics}
A major exception to individual-centered accounts appears in institutional economics, which places the collective and governance dimensions of resilience at the center. In her foundational analysis of common-pool resources, \textcite{ostrom1990governing} showed how communities operating under uncertain and demanding conditions can sustain shared resources through self-organized governance arrangements grounded in trust, reciprocity, monitoring, and graduated sanctions. In this tradition, resilience is not merely recovery or endurance. It is the capacity of a socio-economic system to sustain cooperation under uncertainty, external pressure, and resource constraints. Research on social-ecological systems similarly shows that long-term resilience depends on adaptive rule-making, polycentric governance, and alignment between incentives, actors, and shared resource systems \autocite{ostrom1990governing,nelson2007adaptation}. This tradition treats resilience as an emergent outcome of institutional design and collective action rather than as an individual trait or a purely technical property. What it leaves more open is how these governance principles should be translated into contemporary organizations whose coordination increasingly depends on digital infrastructures and interorganizational dependencies.

\paragraph{Complexity and Socio-technical Systems}
Complexity science and socio-technical systems theory shift the focus once more by emphasizing emergence, self-organization, and nonlinear interaction. Empirical studies of open-source ecosystems treat such arrangements as complex adaptive systems whose growth dynamics and collective productivity can be specified quantitatively \autocite{maillart2008zipf,sornette2014how,maillart2019aristotle,klein2015wikipedia}. Within information systems research, parallel work has reframed digital infrastructures and platforms as the missing substrate of organizing \autocite{tilson2010research,yoo2010research}, with implications for how adaptive configurations recombine under disruption \autocite{elsawy2010seeking}. In this view, resilience arises less from centralized control than from local interaction among heterogeneous actors and feedback mechanisms operating across scales \autocite{levin1998ecosystems}. Agent-based simulation, network analysis, and game-theoretic approaches help explain how system-level resilience emerges from distributed behavior \autocite{albert2000error,gao2016universal}. Research on network robustness shows that scale-free systems may be resilient to random failures yet highly vulnerable to targeted attacks \autocite{albert2000error}. Cross-scale infrastructure work further shows that disruptions frequently cascade across spatial, temporal, and governance levels through self-excited dynamics that propagate beyond the originating perturbation \autocite{aerts2018integrating,saichev2013hierarchy}, and social-ecological systems research stresses the role of institutions, collective learning, and adaptive governance in sustaining resilience. This perspective is especially useful for organizational and digital contexts because it foregrounds distributed adaptation and interdependence. Its drawback is different: it is often easier to identify resilience as an emergent system-level property than to translate that property into clear organizational responsibilities, interventions, or metrics.

Taken together, these traditions define resilience as recovery, reorganization, cognition, governance, or distributed emergence---often simultaneously. Resilience therefore entered organizational research not as a unified construct but as a composite label carrying partly incompatible assumptions about what should persist, who adapts, and how adaptation should be evaluated (Figure~\ref{fig:resilience_phases}).

\subsection{Resilience in Organizations}

Organizational research became interested in resilience for reasons rooted in its own lineage. \textcite{simon1997administrative}'s analysis of bounded rationality and \textcite{march1993organizations}'s account of organizations as cooperative systems under uncertainty established adaptation as a normal administrative problem---visible whenever managers must act on incomplete information about staffing, supply flows, or recovery priorities rather than on fully optimized plans. \textcite{weick2011managing}'s studies of hospitals and other high-reliability settings, together with work on organizational learning, extended that logic into environments where disruption is recurring and coordination must be sustained under strain \autocite{lengnick2011developing}. Events such as the COVID-19 pandemic made the stakes concrete: health systems and firms had to maintain core operations while reconfiguring supply chains, digital coordination, and governance in weeks rather than quarters \autocite{haldane2021health,almeida2020challenges}. When organizational researchers later adopted the term, it offered a label for that enduring concern---how organizations maintain core functions while revising structure, practice, and coordination as conditions change \autocite{bhamra2011resilience,burnard2011organisational}. As Figure~\ref{fig:resilience_phases} shows, they drew selectively on the scientific traditions reviewed above---which carry partly incompatible assumptions---and translated them into managerial terms that can guide design, accountability, and action. They did so in three ways: by specifying structural arrangements and cultural practices such as decentralization, rehearsal, and cross-unit coordination \autocite{bhamra2011resilience,weick2011managing}; by reframing resilience as a dynamic managerial capability---the capacity to sense disruption and reconfigure resources before performance breaks down \autocite{teece2007explicating,duchek2019organizational,sambamurthy2003shaping,pavlou2010elusive}; and, in cybersecurity especially, by embedding it in standards and regulation such as ISO~22301 and the European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act \autocite{calder2021iso,european2022regulation,linkov2013resilience}. That translation was productive because it made resilience actionable in governance routines, standards, and incident-response practice. It was also selective: as \textcite{bhamra2011resilience} and \textcite{burnard2011organisational} note, the same organization can be asked to demonstrate rapid recovery, continuous adaptation, and regulatory compliance without a shared definition of what counts as resilient performance.

\subsubsection{The Construction of Resilience in Organizations}

A large part of the organizational literature explains resilience through two broad sets of mechanisms: structural arrangements \autocite{bhamra2011resilience,burnard2011organisational} and cultural practices \autocite{weick2011managing,westrum2004typology}. Together, these strands describe how organizations prepare for disruption \autocite{bhamra2011resilience,burnard2011organisational}, respond under pressure \autocite{weick2011managing,boin2013resilient}, and learn over time \autocite{lengnick2011developing}.\\

Structural arrangements matter because they shape how control, information, and resources move when conditions become unstable \autocite{galbraith1973designing,burnard2011organisational,bhamra2011resilience}. Decentralized structures can support quicker local responses when disruptions unfold unevenly across units or time horizons \autocite{boin2013resilient}. Modularity can limit cascading failure by isolating breakdowns and preserving partial system functionality \autocite{panteli2017metrics,albert2000error}. Ambidextrous forms of organization can also help firms maintain efficiency while preserving enough flexibility to innovate under pressure \autocite{bhamra2011resilience,teece2007explicating}. These features are especially relevant in digital and cybersecurity settings, where overly centralized coordination can create bottlenecks and increase vulnerability \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience,linkov2013resilience,bjorck2015cyber}.\\

Cultural practices matter no less, and analytically inseparable from the digital systems through which they are now enacted \autocite{orlikowski2007sociomaterial}. Rehearsals, simulation-based training, and pre-mortem analysis help build shared mental models, reduce uncertainty, and strengthen trust under stress \autocite{westrum2004typology,weick2011managing}. Participatory formats such as hackathons and cross-disciplinary innovation sprints can similarly mobilize collective intelligence under time pressure, producing both immediate outputs and longer-term collaborative capacity \autocite{maillart2024computational}. Practices such as improvisation, learning orientation, and psychological safety further support teams facing novel or high-pressure situations \autocite{vogus2007organizational}. In fast-moving threat environments, these practices make it possible to react and learn before formal control systems can be revised \autocite{westrum2004typology}.\\

One of the main contributions of this literature is that it makes resilience organizationally interpretable \autocite{bhamra2011resilience,burnard2011organisational,lengnick2011developing}. Resilience is no longer treated only as a system-level outcome but as a property that can be shaped through deliberate design choices, organizational routines, and cultural conditions \autocite{hepfer2022heterogeneity,weick2011managing,westrum2004typology}. The remaining task is to identify, more precisely, the pathways through which these structural and cultural enablers actually produce adaptive behavior, and the indicators that would confirm whether such behavior is taking place \autocite{vogus2007organizational,hillmann2021organizational,barasa2018what,woods2015four,pavlou2006it,chakravarty2013information,lee2015how}.

\subsubsection{Resilience as a Dynamic Managerial Capability}

A second major line of work treats resilience as a dynamic managerial capability. From this perspective, resilience is less a reactive outcome than a path-dependent capacity formed through routines, governance arrangements, resource deployment, and strategic intent \autocite{lengnick2011developing,boin2013resilient}. This marks a shift away from narrow views of robustness or risk avoidance. The emphasis falls instead on an organization's ability to interpret uncertainty, reconfigure resources, and continue functioning under pressure.\\

Recent work explicitly frames organizational resilience as a form of dynamic capability \autocite{duchek2019organizational,boh2023building,lengnick2011developing} that allows firms to sense disruption \autocite{teece2007explicating,boin2013resilient}, respond effectively \autocite{boin2013resilient}, and in some cases derive advantage from it \autocite{taleb2012antifragile}. In that view, resilience depends on microfoundations such as routines \autocite{teece2007explicating,lengnick2011developing}, heuristics, and cognitive schemas that shape how actors interpret events and act under uncertainty \autocite{weick2011managing,hepfer2022heterogeneity}. Resilience therefore cannot be reduced to resistance or continuity alone \autocite{woods2015four,holling1996engineering}. It requires an adaptive orientation embedded in both operational and strategic action \autocite{hepfer2022heterogeneity,annarelli2016strategic,nambisan2017digital}.\\

In practice, that capability is usually supported by combinations of managerial levers. Slack resources and redundancy can provide buffering capacity \autocite{barasa2018what}. Ambidextrous arrangements can support the simultaneous pursuit of stability and innovation \autocite{bhamra2011resilience}. Resilience audits and readiness diagnostics aim to assess whether organizations can absorb and adapt to shocks \autocite{burnard2011organisational,calder2021iso}. This research stream is useful because it translates resilience into a language managers can act on. What it still does not resolve is a basic problem: resilience is often described as a desirable capability before there is clear agreement on what observable performance would actually demonstrate that the capability exists \autocite{woods2015four,linkov2013resilience,hillmann2021organizational}.

\subsubsection{Cybersecurity Resilience as a Strategic and Institutional Imperative}

Cybersecurity is a particularly revealing setting because many of the broader organizational tensions around resilience become more visible \autocite{bjorck2015cyber,dupont2019cyberresilience,boin2013resilient}. Cyber risks are marked by extreme uncertainty \autocite{maillart2010heavy,wheatley2016extreme}, rapid change \autocite{kuypers2018designing}, and interdependent vulnerabilities that cut across technical, human, and organizational layers \autocite{linkov2013resilience,falco2019cyber,bjorck2015cyber}. They are also often asymmetric: threats may emerge quickly \autocite{hutchins2011intelligence}, evolve during response \autocite{hausken2020cyber}, and remain only partly visible until damage is already underway \autocite{bilge2012before}. Under such conditions, compliance-oriented and control-oriented approaches are not sufficient \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}. This is one reason cybersecurity is now increasingly treated not only as a control problem, but as a resilience problem \autocite{linkov2013resilience,dupont2019cyberresilience}.\\

The cyber-resilience literature generally presents resilience as an integrated capability combining technological safeguards, strategic foresight, organizational adaptability, and institutional alignment \autocite{bjorck2015cyber,lostri2022shared}. Standards such as ISO 22301 emphasize the maintenance of core functions under disruption \autocite{calder2021iso}, while other work places more weight on learning, reflexive governance, and ongoing adjustment \autocite{hausken2020cyber}. Regulatory pressures, including those associated with Basel III and related resilience initiatives in finance, have further reinforced resilience as a strategic imperative by tying it to legitimacy, continuity, and systemic risk management \autocite{almeida2020challenges}. This has contributed to a broader shift in governance: firms are increasingly judged not only by the strength of their own security posture, but also by the extent to which they help sustain resilience across wider ecosystems \autocite{bjorck2015cyber,lostri2022shared}.\\

Standards and regulation have institutionalized resilience around this industry backbone---BCPs, impact tolerances, ICT continuity, and threat-led testing in finance \autocite{bcbs2021operational,calder2021iso,european2022regulation,ecb2018tiber}---yet assurance remains document-centered: compliance is easier to demonstrate than adaptive performance under disruption \autocite{hillmann2021organizational,woods2015four}. Field interviews corroborate that pattern (\textit{``\QuoteCompliance{}''}; Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}). The remainder of the review addresses the resulting divergence among regulatory, managerial, and socio-technical renderings of resilience in digital settings \autocite{boin2013resilient,linkov2013resilience}.
