\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:Conclusion}

Resilience has become a prominent ambition in management and information systems, yet it remains more often proclaimed than enacted. Organizations invoke the term widely, but in practice they face conceptual ambiguity, methodological gaps, and limited empirical grounding. These weaknesses produce a compliance-oriented version of resilience that signals legitimacy without cultivating adaptive capability.\\
\\
This review identified three core limitations. First, construct clarity remains weak: resilience is frequently conflated with robustness, agility, or redundancy, leaving its distinctive mechanisms underspecified. Second, operationalization is limited: organizations and scholars alike lack valid indicators that capture adaptive performance under disruption. Third, cumulative evidence is scarce: findings are fragmented across domains, preventing generalizable lessons from emerging.\\
\\
Building on these gaps, we outlined a research agenda that treats resilience as a measurable socio-technical capability. Four directions are central. Operational indicators should make resilience observable through behavioral and computational markers. Integrative design principles must connect routines, infrastructures, and governance arrangements. Benchmarks and early-warning systems are needed to align incentives and prevent “metrics theater.” Finally, comparative and cumulative evidence, combining simulation with field data, is essential to establish transferable insights across levels of analysis.\\
\\
By moving resilience from rhetorical ideal to tractable construct, information systems research can provide both theoretical rigor and managerial relevance. The field is well positioned to define resilience not as symbolic compliance, but as a genuine capability for adaptation in complex digital environments.
