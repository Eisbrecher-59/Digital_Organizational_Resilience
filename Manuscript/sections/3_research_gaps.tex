\section{\MakeUppercase {Research Gaps}}
\label{sec:RG}
\renewcommand{\baselinestretch}{2.0}\normalsize

Resilience is now a recurring expectation in policy documents, strategy discussions, and academic work, and a growing body of substantive contributions has matured around it. The literature is rich rather than thin: engineering, ecology, psychology, and complex systems have each produced careful work on resilience, though they describe different phenomena and proceed from different assumptions. Most of these traditions were not originally developed for organization-level analysis. When resilience is brought into managerial and regulatory settings, that divergence becomes a practical question of translation. Organizations are asked to demonstrate resilience and have invested considerable effort in doing so, yet shared anchors for what that should mean in practice, how it should be measured, and how it should be governed are still being developed \autocite{hillmann2021organizational}.\\

Part of the problem is semantic, but only part. Across disciplines, resilience has been used to refer to recovery, robustness, adaptation, learning, and transformation. These labels are often treated as adjacent, sometimes even as interchangeable, yet they rest on different views of time, change, and system behavior. A recovery-oriented account assumes that disruption is followed by return. A transformative account does not. It allows for the possibility that disruption changes the system more fundamentally. When these perspectives are carried into organizational settings, they do not produce the same priorities. A firm may invest in robustness and then be evaluated on adaptability, or build compliance structures while being told it should become more transformative.\\

This review argues that three related problems follow from that situation: (i) conceptual plurality without operational convergence, (ii) limited empirical validation of adaptive performance, and (iii) methodological fragmentation across domains. The first problem is conceptual. Management and information systems research still lacks a shared operational core for studying resilience. The second is empirical. Resilience is rarely examined through direct observation of how organizations adapt over time. The third is methodological. Existing metrics and formal models remain only weakly connected to the governance and evaluation systems organizations actually use. Of these, the issue of operationalizable and transferable measures is especially important \autocite{woods2015four}. Similar concerns have been raised in fields such as infrastructure and healthcare \autocite{heinimann2017infrastructure,kruk2017building}. Details of the review process are reported in Appendix~\ref{appendix:B}, and Appendix~\ref{appendix:C} summarizes how these tensions appear in organizational practice.\\
\\
\textbf{Conceptual plurality without operational convergence}. Resilience has been conceptualized through lenses of mechanical stability, ecological transformation, cognitive adaptability, and socio-technical flexibility (see Appendix~\ref{appendix:A}). Each of these perspectives offers useful and often rigorously developed insights, but their assumptions are not equivalent. Engineering resilience emphasizes restoration of predefined functions. Ecological resilience foregrounds regime shifts and adaptive reorganization. Psychological resilience centers on coping, endurance, and growth under stress. Socio-technical perspectives highlight emergence, interdependence, and distributed coordination. These perspectives can be read as complementary at a high level, but their implications for organizational design, measurement, and accountability differ substantially.\\

In organizational practice, this plurality can produce definitional drift. Terms such as resistance, robustness, recovery, agility, and adaptation are sometimes used as though they referred to the same thing, even when they point to different mechanisms and temporal dynamics. As a result, resilience is often articulated through strategy documents, audits, and compliance templates---important instruments in their own right---while the underlying adaptive capability is still being developed in parallel \autocite{duchek2019organizational}. The same difficulty surfaces in our own field interviews (Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}, theme i and Table~\ref{tab:RotaResult}), where senior practitioners report being unable to specify operational metrics for the resilience they are nonetheless asked to demonstrate (\textit{``We talk about resilience in every committee, but nobody can show me how we measure it''}). A clearer account of how these conceptual perspectives translate into observable organizational mechanisms would help the field move from a conceptually rich position to one that is also operationally precise.\\
\\
\textbf{Limited empirical validation and longitudinal grounding}. Empirical studies in cybersecurity and risk management indicate that resilience is most commonly assessed through audits, maturity models, and certifications. These instruments have substantially advanced organizational accountability, and they are increasingly complemented by scenario-based exercises, table-top simulations, and red-teaming practices that have been refined over more than two decades \autocite{skopik2016problem}. Where opportunity for further development lies is in the systematic observation of adaptive performance during disruption (simulated or real), and in connecting these advanced tools—predictive simulations, anomaly detection, and exercise-based learning—to regulatory evaluation structures. Equally promising is a more deliberate effort to render visible those informal practices that already prove crucial to adaptive response in many organizations: parallel communication channels, crisis improvisation, and decentralized escalation \autocite{boin2013resilient,duchek2019organizational}.\\

These observations become more concrete when examining the instruments organizations actually rely on. In the European financial sector, the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) \autocite{european2022regulation} consolidates ICT risk management, incident classification and reporting, third-party ICT risk oversight, and threat-led penetration testing into a single binding regime applicable since January 2025. The NIS2 Directive \autocite{european2022nis2} extends comparable obligations across a much broader set of essential and important entities, with explicit accountability for management bodies. National regulators have moved in parallel: FINMA Circular 2023/1 on operational risks and resilience \autocite{finma2023operational} requires Swiss banks to identify critical functions, set tolerances for disruption, and maintain scenario-based recovery plans, while the Basel Committee's \emph{Principles for Operational Resilience} \autocite{bcbs2021operational} generalize the approach internationally by requiring identification of critical operations, mapping of interconnections and interdependencies, and scenario testing against severe-but-plausible disruptions. At the management-systems level, ISO 22301 \autocite{calder2021iso} codifies business continuity management, ISO/IEC 27001 \autocite{iso27001} codifies information security management, and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 \autocite{nist2024csf} offers a widely adopted reference architecture organized around the Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover functions. Together, these instruments represent a substantial and largely convergent effort to make resilience an auditable property of organizations.\\

What these frameworks capture well is the presence and design of resilience-relevant controls, the documentation of decision authority, and the existence of recovery objectives. What is less directly captured is adaptive performance \emph{during} disruption: the speed with which cross-functional teams reorganize when a scenario diverges from plan; the conditions under which informal escalation channels are activated and respected; the half-life of post-incident lessons in routines and tooling; or the interaction between an organization's own response and the simultaneous responses of partners, suppliers, and infrastructure providers. Most current instruments are designed for periodic point-in-time evaluation, which complements rather than replaces the continuous longitudinal observation that adaptive capacity arguably requires. Field evidence from our interviews (Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}, theme ii) is consistent with this reading: respondents described much of their resilience work as oriented toward demonstrating compliance to auditors rather than toward direct preparation for incidents (\textit{``Most of our resilience work is to show auditors we are compliant---not to prepare for real incidents''}), and they distinguished sharply between the value of formal frameworks for accountability and their limits as predictors of how the organization will actually behave when disturbed.\\

A parallel observation applies to the testing apparatus that has matured around these frameworks. Penetration testing and ethical hacking, formalized through standards such as the Penetration Testing Execution Standard and the OWASP testing guides, have been routine practice in large organizations since the early 2000s. Threat-intelligence-led red-teaming has since added an adversary-emulation layer: the Bank of England's CBEST programme \autocite{boe2021cbest}, the European Central Bank's TIBER-EU framework \autocite{ecb2018tiber}, and DORA's threat-led penetration testing requirement now formalize this practice for financial-sector firms across Europe. Sector- and union-wide cyber exercises such as ENISA's biennial Cyber Europe \autocite{enisa2024cybereurope} complement firm-level testing with scenario-based stress tests of cross-organizational coordination, and internal table-top exercises and crisis-management drills are now standard in most regulated firms. These programmes have substantially advanced what organizations know about their technical attack surface, their detection-and-response pipelines, and the readiness of named decision-makers under pre-defined scenarios. Where they capture resilience only partially is in the dimensions that matter most when disruption diverges from script: how decision rights are reallocated when the named incident commander is unavailable; how coordination is maintained when communication channels degrade; how teams improvise when the playbook does not match the situation; and whether near-misses produce durable organizational learning rather than after-action reports that are filed and not revisited. Existing exercises are typically scored against completion of pre-defined steps rather than against the variability, speed, and reconfiguration of response---the very signatures that the resilience literature treats as constitutive of adaptive capacity \autocite{woods2015four,duchek2019organizational}. Our field interviews (Appendix~\ref{appendix:C}, theme v) reflect the same intuition: respondents valued cyber-range exercises and table-tops, but flagged that their training value depends on tight coupling to daily practice (\textit{``Cyber ranges are nice exercises, but unless linked to daily practice, they remain theater''}). The point is not that these instruments fall short of their stated objectives, which they generally meet; it is that resilience as an enacted socio-technical capability has properties that an additional layer of observation, measurement, and analysis can usefully render visible.\\

A similar pattern appears in healthcare and public health systems. Resilience is actively practiced there, and pioneering measurement frameworks are now being extended to capture more of the adaptive work involved \autocite{kruk2017building}. Longitudinal studies tracing how resilience evolves across repeated disruptions are growing, with notable examples in disaster recovery, healthcare reform, and infrastructure governance \autocite{allen2018quantifying} that offer useful blueprints for further work. The remaining frontier concerns the uncertainty, reversibility, and trade-offs involved in resilience assessment, where existing frameworks could be extended further. Greater cross-disciplinary translation would also help: analytical methods developed in control theory or complex systems have so far migrated only selectively into organizational research, and widely used practices such as awareness campaigns could be strengthened by tighter coupling with measurable behavioral indicators \autocite{bada2019cyber}.
\\
To examine how resilience research is distributed across domains and methodological approaches, we systematically classified 89 publications by thematic focus (rows) and analytical method (columns). The resulting cross-disciplinary map (Table~\ref{tab:ResearchGaps}) makes the asymmetries visible at a glance. Simulation \& Modeling and Network Analysis are well populated in Complex Adaptive Systems, Engineering, and Climate \& Disaster Management \autocite{wiener1948cybernetics,holling1973resilience,albert2000error,dawson2011agentbased,gao2016universal,panteli2017metrics}, where formal models of perturbation and recovery have been developed for decades; Markers \& Metrics show concentrated activity in Engineering \autocite{bruneau2003framework,francis2014metric} and Public Health \autocite{cutter2010disaster,kruk2017building}, where indicator design has explicit policy uptake. The same management rows are densely populated in Surveys and Case Studies, where conceptual and capability-oriented work has matured \autocite{vogus2007organizational,weick2011managing,lengnick2011developing,burnard2011organisational,boin2013resilient,bjorck2015cyber,duchek2019organizational,calder2021iso,boh2023building}. The pattern is consistent with the gaps identified above: management and risk research has built strong qualitative and conceptual foundations; the next stage of work concerns extending the quantitative measurement, simulation, and network-analytical methods already familiar in adjacent fields into organization-level governance.\\

\textbf{Methodological fragmentation and limited transfer}. This gap becomes especially visible in the cross-disciplinary classification of the 89 resilience-related publications summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ResearchGaps}. Simulation models, network analyses, and quantitative resilience metrics are well established in engineering, complex systems, and climate research \autocite{francis2014metric}. Their systematic integration into domains central to organizational and managerial resilience, such as cybersecurity, risk management, and strategic governance, is at an earlier stage. Table~\ref{tab:ResearchGaps} shows that \textit{Markers \& Metrics} and \textit{Technology} are comparatively less represented in these fields, and that simulation-based approaches are only beginning to be incorporated into organization-level governance frameworks \autocite{linkov2013resilience,kuypers2018designing}. As a result, organizations and scholars continue to rely on maturity models, expert judgment, and audit documentation, which provide essential accountability while leaving room to develop methods that complement them with finer-grained observation of adaptive performance under real-world conditions.\\

Even within cybersecurity, formal models of attack--defense dynamics exist \autocite{aydin2018integration}, yet they are seldom integrated into broader organizational resilience architectures. Other transferable approaches, including ecological network analysis \autocite{luke2012systems}, transportation recovery modeling \autocite{jacobs2017applying}, and agent-based infrastructure risk models \autocite{dawson2011agentbased}, also remain underused in digital resilience planning. The problem is therefore not methodological scarcity. It is the weak translation of technical advances in resilience measurement into governance tools that organizations can actually use \autocite{heinimann2017infrastructure,aerts2018integrating}.\\
\\
These three areas are interconnected. Conceptual plurality shapes how measurement evolves; partial empirical validation conditions how cumulative evidence accumulates; methodological fragmentation slows the transfer of analytical tools across domains. The encouraging implication is that resilience research already holds substantial theoretical and methodological resources; the next stage is to integrate them more tightly so that they support cumulative, organization-ready application. Standardized, validated, and scalable metrics embedded in governance structures would help resilience develop from a strong organizing principle into a systematically cultivated capability.\\
\\
Mathematical approaches from dynamical systems theory, such as basin stability, return dynamics, and perturbation--response analysis, provide promising foundations for formally characterizing resilience and tracing system adaptation over time \autocite{krakovska2023resilience}. As organizational processes increasingly depend on interconnected digital infrastructures, data flows, and platform-based ecosystems, these approaches offer a useful basis for modeling how adaptive capacity emerges and evolves within digitally mediated organizational systems.

\subsection{From Organizational Resilience to Digital Organizational Resilience}

Organizational resilience has traditionally referred to a firm's capacity to absorb shocks, adapt to disruption, and sustain core functions, typically explained through managerial capabilities, organizational learning, and structural flexibility \autocite{westrum2004typology,vogus2007organizational,weick2011managing,bhamra2011resilience,burnard2011organisational,lengnick2011developing,duchek2019organizational}. Contemporary organizations, however, operate through digitally mediated processes whose adaptive capacity is shaped not only by managerial decisions but also by technological architectures, data flows, and inter-organizational coordination mechanisms \autocite{henfridsson2013generative,liu2022network,hepfer2022heterogeneity,gillard2023efficient,boh2023building}. Building on this body of work---and in particular on accounts that already treat digital infrastructures as constitutive of organizational adaptation \autocite{henfridsson2013generative,liu2022network,hepfer2022heterogeneity}, on dynamic-capability framings of resilience \autocite{teece2007explicating,duchek2019organizational,boh2023building}, and on the institutional and governance traditions that situate adaptive action within rule and authority structures \autocite{ostrom1990governing,boin2013resilient,linkov2013resilience}---we adopt the term \emph{digital organizational resilience} to refer specifically to the socio-technical specification of organizational resilience in digitally mediated environments. The label is not a claim to a separate theoretical domain: it consolidates these existing strands by foregrounding that adaptive capacity in contemporary organizations is distributed across digital infrastructures, institutional arrangements, and inter-organizational ecosystems, and that its realization depends on infrastructural interdependencies and the governance arrangements that make them workable \autocite{janssen2006network,he2022building}.\\

\subsubsection{Three lines of work, one missing synthesis}

Research on digital resilience has matured along three distinguishable but partial lines, and naming the differences between them helps clarify what an integrative framework still has to do. The first line examines the resilience of digital infrastructures and technological systems: how architectures, redundancy mechanisms, and reconfiguration capacities sustain technical services during disturbance. Cyber-resilience research provides empirically grounded accounts of detection, response, and recovery \autocite{linkov2013resilience,bjorck2015cyber,dupont2019cyberresilience,hausken2020cyber}; network analysis characterizes topological robustness, scale-free vulnerabilities, and universal recovery dynamics \autocite{albert2000error,gao2016universal,panteli2017metrics}; and formal models of attack--defense dynamics quantify trade-offs between security investment and exposure \autocite{aydin2018integration,kuypers2018designing,ibnehossain2020modeling}. This work treats organizations primarily as operators of technical systems. The second line approaches resilience as an organizational capability, foregrounding leadership, mindfulness, governance, and learning routines: from Weick and Sutcliffe's high-reliability tradition \autocite{vogus2007organizational,weick2011managing} to capability- and dynamic-capability-oriented accounts \autocite{teece2007explicating,bhamra2011resilience,lengnick2011developing,duchek2019organizational,boh2023building}. It offers detailed accounts of internal mechanisms but is lighter on the technological infrastructures through which those mechanisms now operate. The third line addresses the ecosystem level, examining how information sharing, platform governance, and collaborative incident response generate collective adaptive capacity \autocite{arnold2004informationsharing,janssen2006network,enisa2017isacs,liu2022network,lostri2022shared,gillard2023efficient}; it captures coordinated action across organizational boundaries but does less to connect ecosystem-level dynamics back to internal organizational mechanisms. Each line is substantive in its own right. What remains undeveloped is the synthesis that links them: an account of how technological, managerial, and collaborative mechanisms interact across organizational, infrastructural, and ecosystem scopes to produce, or fail to produce, adaptive performance in digitally mediated settings. The Enactment-Scope framework introduced next supplies that connecting structure.
