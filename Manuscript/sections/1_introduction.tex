\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:Introduction}
\renewcommand{\baselinestretch}{2.0}\normalsize

The release of Anthropic's Mythos in 2026, a cyber-focused generative artificial intelligence (AI) model that can identify software vulnerabilities and draft exploits faster than most security teams, sent large organizations scrambling. Security leaders warned that such capabilities could enable automated mass exploitation that outpaces enterprise patching \autocite{smith2026mythos}. Beneath the alarm, the episode exposes a recurring organizational challenge: each new wave of digital tools, {\it a fortiori} recent AI advances, compresses the time available to revise governance, controls, and cross-unit coordination before prior adaptations have taken hold \autocite{paries2017resilience}.

These developments reflect a broader shift in the conditions under which organizations operate. As digital technologies have become more deeply embedded in organizational processes, coordination, and interdependence, disruptions increasingly arise not only from technical failures, but also from human practices and their interaction with technical systems \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}. Such disruptions cannot be addressed through technical safeguards or formal policies alone. They require organizations to build capabilities for adaptation and coordination across intertwined human and technical components under conditions of uncertainty \autocite{boin2013resilient}. Resilience therefore no longer concerns only response to crises, but also the organizational ability to adjust, reorganize, and sustain operations as disturbances unfold at various frequency and magnitude \autocite{burnard2011organisational}.

Against this backdrop, resilience has become a central regulatory priority \autocite{bcbs2021operational}. Crises such as COVID-19 \autocite{almeida2020challenges}, large-scale cyber incidents \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}, and systemic information technology failures have moved resilience from an aspirational organizational goal toward a governance commitment \autocite{boin2013resilient}. Across sectors ranging from finance to healthcare to critical infrastructure, organizations are now expected to anticipate, absorb, and adapt disruptions while maintaining operational continuity and legitimacy. This expectation is reinforced by landmark regulatory initiatives such as the European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and Australia's Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018, which formalize resilience requirements in sectors exposed to digital and cyber disruption \autocite{SOCI2018,european2022regulation}. These initiatives represent a significant collective effort by regulators, standard-setting bodies, and organizations to make resilience an explicit object of governance. At the same time, practitioners report that organizations often find it difficult to adapt at the pace of an accelerating and unstable technological environment \autocite{paries2017resilience}. A persistent gap therefore remains between regulatory ambition and organizational capability: managers continue to grapple with what resilience means for their specific context, and with how to measure and operationalize it \autocite{bjorck2015cyber}. Organizations also increasingly face disruptions that are continuous, novel, and overlapping, whereas many existing assurance systems were originally designed for a limited set of exceptional shocks. As a result, audit-driven compliance and control frameworks —- important as they are for accountability -— tend to emphasize documentation and formal assurance at a point in time, and provide a more limited window onto the learning, coordination, and flexible response that adaptive capacity also requires across successive disruptions \autocite{power1999audit,woods2015four}.\\

These tensions are not confined to the literature; they are also visible in organizational practice. To expose the relevance of the review to practitioners, we draw on an exploratory qualitative field study of 18 semi-structured interviews with senior cybersecurity and risk professionals from large organizations \autocite{rota2024} (see Appendix~\ref{appendix:C} for study design, aggregated sample characteristics, interview protocol, and thematic results). The interviews highlight the genuine effort organizations devote to resilience and surface six recurring themes (Table~\ref{tab:RotaResult}): (i) difficulty defining and quantifying resilience, (ii) regulatory expectations and demonstrable compliance, (iii) coordination challenges across organizational silos, (iv) limited conversion of incidents into organizational learning, (v) uneven integration of human and cultural factors, and (vi) tension between regulatory flexibility and organizational demands for precision. These observations reinforce a central point of this review: resilience is widely prescribed and seriously pursued, but the means of enacting it as an organizational capability remain uneven.\\

These practical difficulties are accompanied by a notable plurality of meaning in the scientific literature. As resilience has developed across disciplines and industries, its referents have multiplied, spanning system uptime, business continuity, regulatory posture, and security architecture \autocite{woods2015four,hillmann2021organizational}. Each of these usages reflects a substantive contribution to the field, but the variety also creates challenges for conceptual coherence and practical accountability. Even where organizations adopt established resilience and risk-management practices -- such as audits, business continuity planning, incident response procedures, or post-incident reviews -- the conversion of incidents and near-misses into sustained organizational learning still depends on supporting routines for reflexivity, decentralization, and cross-unit coordination that are themselves under active development \autocite{francis2014metric,barasa2018what}. A growing body of standards and guidelines now provides important reference points for resilience, and the next step is to complement them with methods that quantify, simulate, design, and implement resilience in ways that organizations can routinely apply \autocite{kuypers2018designing}. These efforts are made more demanding by the fact that resilience has been translated into organizational settings through partially divergent assumptions about stability, adaptation, control, and transformation.\\
\\
Advancing resilience in organizational settings therefore requires an integrative account of how adaptive capacity is enacted across socio-technical arrangements. This paper develops that account as a cross-disciplinary review of resilience research, and organizes its findings through the Enactment--Scope framework---offered not as a new theory of resilience but as a device for structuring a research agenda for digital organizational resilience. Synthesizing that literature with the coordination, learning, and compliance pressures identified in our practitioner interviews, the framework specifies \emph{how} resilience is produced---through technological, managerial, and collaborative mechanisms---and \emph{where} it operates---across infrastructural, organizational, and ecosystem scopes---while clarifying the translation mechanisms (operational indicators, design principles, and benchmarks and warnings) through which stated commitment becomes observable capability. This reframing explains why translation from concept to practice repeatedly stalls when initiatives address only one mechanism or scope in isolation, and shifts analysis from what resilience ought to mean to where adaptive capacity is located, how it is enacted, and what conditions support or constrain it under disruption. It also yields three concrete research directions: making enactment observable through behavioral and computational indicators and simulation-based stress testing; analyzing how resilience mechanisms distribute and interact across organizational and institutional scopes; and tracing how enacted capacity accumulates, erodes, or is redirected through successive disruptions.
