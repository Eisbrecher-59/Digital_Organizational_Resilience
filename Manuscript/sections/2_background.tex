\section{Background}
\label{sec:Background}

This section critically examines how resilience has been conceptualized across disciplines and taken up in management and information systems research. It shows that the concept’s rapid diffusion across technical, social, and organizational domains has produced not merely diversity, but a fragmentation of meanings that actively hinders cumulative theory and practical enactment. By juxtaposing these perspectives, the section clarifies the sources of ambiguity that later translate into persistent gaps in measurement, governance, and organizational implementation.

\subsection{A short history of resilience across disciplines}

%Resilience broadly refers to the capacity of a system to absorb disruption while maintaining or regaining function. Yet this core idea has taken on different meanings depending on disciplinary focus and context. Across fields, resilience has been used to describe not only recovery and stability, but also adaptation, learning, and transformation. However, its widespread adoption has also led to conceptual ambiguity, with inconsistent definitions hindering its effective application across domains \autocite{alexander2013resilience}. Efforts to address this have included typologies that map competing interpretations and highlight the need for context-specific operationalization \autocite{meerow2016defining}. Moreover, in policy and planning contexts, resilience is sometimes invoked in ways that prioritize rhetorical appeal over analytical clarity, further blurring its meaning and limiting its translation into actionable strategies \autocite{gibbs2013beyond}. This section synthesizes five core disciplinary perspectives on resilience: mechanical, ecological, systemic, complex, and psychological -- while referring the reader to Appendix \ref{appendix:A} for technical details.\\

The earliest foundations of resilience emerge from the physical and mathematical sciences, where it is understood as a system’s capacity to absorb disturbance and return to equilibrium. Early formulations in physics and systems thinking emphasized elasticity, stability, and homeostatic feedback as defining features of resilient behavior \autocite{rankine1858manual, wiener1948cybernetics, panteli2017metrics}. In this view, resilience is fundamentally about mechanical rebound and temporal recovery. These ideas provided the basis for formal, quantitative treatments of resilience, particularly in engineered systems, where disturbance-response dynamics can be mathematically specified and measured.\\

Building on these foundations, biology and ecology fundamentally altered the meaning of resilience by shifting attention away from equilibrium restoration toward persistence through variability and regime change. In this view, resilience no longer implies return to a prior state but the capacity to reorganize while preserving core functions \autocite{holling1973resilience, gunderson2002panarchy}. This vision emphasized that resilience is multi-scalar and emerges from feedbacks across ecological, social, and institutional levels \autocite{sundstrom2014transdisciplinary}. Rather than returning to a single stable state, resilient systems may adapt or transform while preserving core functions. Ecological models such as the adaptive cycle highlight transitions between stability regimes and the role of diversity and redundancy as buffers against systemic collapse \autocite{walker2004resilience, folke2004regime, folke2010resilience}. This tradition also introduced stochastic and probabilistic approaches to resilience, focusing on recovery under random disturbances rather than deterministic shocks. Early metrics relied on return variance, autocorrelation, and recovery time to quantify resilience under uncertainty \autocite{ives1995measuring} (see Appendix \ref{appendix:A2}). In digital infrastructure contexts, this implies designing for flexible reconfiguration under pressure rather than assuming recovery to a predefined baseline.\\

While these perspectives are often presented as complementary, they embed fundamentally different assumptions about stability, control, and desirable outcomes. Engineering resilience presumes the recoverability of a known system, whereas ecological resilience accepts irreversibility, nonlinearity, and transformation as normal conditions. The literature rarely addresses how these divergent assumptions can be reconciled when resilience is invoked as a single organizational objective.\\

Insights from neuroscience and psychology further anthropicized the concept by framing resilience as the capacity of individuals and groups to endure, adapt, and grow through adversity \autocite{luthar2000construct, kalisch2024neurobiology}. Rooted in research on trauma, stress response, and emotional regulation, this literature emphasizes cognitive flexibility, learning, and neural plasticity as core mechanisms of resilience \autocite{feder2019biology, masten2010disasters}. Biological studies link resilience to efficient stress regulation systems, notably the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, which governs physiological recovery after stress exposure \autocite{mcewen2004protection}. Epigenetic research further suggests that life experiences can shape the expression of resilience-related genes, highlighting the plasticity of adaptive capacity over time \autocite{rakesh2019resilience}. In organizational settings -- particularly cybersecurity operations, healthcare, and emergency response -- these insights underscore the importance of adaptive decision-making, emotional regulation under pressure, and team-based resilience competencies \autocite{norris2008community, kruk2017building, haldane2021health}. Widely used psychometric instruments, such as the Wagnild and Young Scale \autocite{wagnild1993development} and the Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale \autocite{campbell2007psychometric}, operationalize these capacities at the individual and group levels. Extending beyond individual-centric models, \textcite{ungar2021modeling} conceptualizes resilience as a multisystemic phenomenon distributed across personal, social, institutional, and ecological contexts.\\

Extending beyond individual-centric and psychosocial models, 
institutional economics introduces a crucial collective dimension of resilience. In her foundational analysis of common-pool resources, \textcite{ostrom1990governing} demonstrates that communities can sustainably manage shared resources through self-organized governance arrangements grounded in trust, reciprocity, monitoring, and graduated sanctions. Resilience in this context is not reducible to recovery or endurance; it is the capacity of a social system to sustain cooperative equilibria under conditions of uncertainty, external pressure, and resource constraints. Research on social–ecological systems further emphasizes that long-term resilience depends on adaptive rule-making, polycentric governance, and alignment between incentives, actors, and shared resource systems \autocite{ostrom1990governing, lebel2006governance}. This perspective introduces a critical economic insight: resilience is embedded in the institutional structures that organize collective action and mediate human interaction with environmental and infrastructural commons. Rather than treating resilience as an individual trait or a purely technical attribute, the commons tradition conceptualizes it as an emergent outcome of cooperative governance that enables societies to remain embedded within ecological limits while preserving adaptive capacity over time.\\

Control theory and engineering then reintroduced resilience into applied domains, particularly infrastructure, safety, and risk management. Here, resilience is often defined as the ability of a system to recover functionality after disruption, commonly measured through recovery time, reliability, and functional uptime \autocite{bruneau2003framework}. A foundational contribution from systems engineering distinguishes resilience as recovery speed, reliability as frequency of success, and vulnerability as severity of failure \autocite{hashimoto1982reliability}. This equilibrium-centric view remains influential in domains where rapid restoration is the primary objective (see Appendix \ref{appendix:A1}). In resilience engineering, Hollnagel reframed this perspective by emphasizing four core capabilities—responding, monitoring, learning, and anticipating -- as practical levers for improving system resilience \autocite{hollnagel2013resilience}. However, in cybersecurity and digital systems, such control-oriented models are increasingly challenged, as adversarial dynamics, rapid evolution, and non-stationary threat landscapes prevent systems from returning to a single known state \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}.\\

Finally, complexity science and socio-technical systems theory extend these perspectives by emphasizing emergence, self-organization, and nonlinear interaction. From this view, resilience arises not from centralized control but from local interactions among heterogeneous agents and feedback mechanisms across scales \autocite{levin1998ecosystems, krakovska2024resilience}. Modeling approaches such as agent-based simulations, network theory, game theory, and thermodynamic analogies help explain how system-level resilience emerges from distributed behaviors \autocite{myerson2013game, albert2000error, gao2016universal}. Research on network robustness shows that scale-free systems are resilient to random failures yet vulnerable to targeted attacks, a finding with direct implications for the design of socio-technical and digital infrastructures \autocite{albert2000error}. Cross-scale infrastructure studies further demonstrate that resilience must be understood across spatial, temporal, and governance levels, as disruptions often cascade across sectors \autocite{moreno2018womens}. Social-ecological systems research complements this view by highlighting the role of institutions, collective learning, adaptive governance, and actor–resource–rule configurations in sustaining resilience \autocite{berkes2000linking, anderies2004framework, adger2005socialecological}. The structure of social networks further conditions resilience by shaping information flows, coordination capacity, and collective action \autocite{bodin2009role}. Recent work emphasizes that resilience strategies inevitably involve political, social, and economic trade-offs \autocite{leichenko2024climate}, with empirical evidence from disaster risk reduction showing how these choices are embedded in broader socio-economic processes \autocite{aase2020resilience}. As detailed in Appendix \ref{appendix:A4}, this perspective frames resilience as an emergent, systemic property rather than a controllable attribute.\\

Taken together, these perspectives reveal resilience as a multidimensional construct whose analytical coherence depends on unresolved trade-offs between recovery, adaptation, and transformation. While they differ in scope and assumptions, they converge on a central insight: resilient systems sustain function not by resisting disruption, but by reorganizing adaptively in response to it. This synthesis sets the stage for understanding how management and information systems research reinterpret resilience not merely as a systemic property, but as a dynamic organizational capability—developed in the next section.

\subsection{Resilience in Organizations}
The physical, ecological, psychological, engineering, and socio-technical characteristics of resilience foreground it as an emergent property of complex systems shaped by interaction, governance, and adaptation rather than by centralized control. This understanding of resilience raises a critical and largely unresolved question for management and information systems research: how an emergent system property can be translated into deliberate organizational action under conditions of uncertainty \autocite{lengnick2011developing, duchek2020organizational}. Here, we examine how management theory currently addresses this challenge by reframing resilience as an organizational capability embedded in decision-making, structural design, and strategic action.\\

\subsection{Design, Culture, and the Construction of Resilience}

Beyond dynamic capabilities, organizational resilience is sustained through institutional arrangements that structure decision-making, coordination, and collective interpretation. These arrangements can be broadly understood along two interdependent dimensions: structural design mechanisms and cultural practices, which together shape how resilience is enacted and maintained over time.\\

Structural design mechanisms enable resilient responses by distributing control, buffering risk and supporting adaptive recombination. Decentralized structures facilitate local adaptation and timely decision-making during crises, particularly when disruptions unfold unpredictably concomitantly,  or asynchronously \autocite{boin2013resilient}. Modularity increases fault tolerance by isolating failures and enabling partial system functionality, while ambidextrous designs allow organizations to balance efficiency with exploration, maintaining operational stability alongside innovation under pressure \autocite{bhamra2011resilience}. These design features are especially salient in cybersecurity contexts, where centralized control can create bottlenecks while modular recovery can limit cascading failures.\\

Cultural practices play an equally central role. Rehearsals, simulation-based training, and pre-mortem analysis foster shared mental models, align expectations, and build trust under stress \autocite{klein2008performing, weick2011managing, westrum2004typology}. Complementary practices such as improvisation, learning orientation, and psychological safety further equip teams to operate effectively in novel or high-pressure situations \autocite{vogus2007organizational, lanzara1999transient}. In fast-moving digital threat environments, resilient cultures support improvisational decision-making and fast-cycle learning, enabling organizations to respond before formal controls can be recalibrated \autocite{duchek2020organizational, westrum2004typology}.\\

Resilience emerges not from any single structural or cultural element, but from their interaction and alignment over time. It is co-constructed through the coordination of human judgment, organizational routines, and technological affordances, which together shape how organizations perceive disruption, reconfigure resources, and mobilize collective action \autocite{henfridsson2013generative, vogus2007organizational, lengnick2011developing}. Taken together, these structural and cultural enablers constitute the organizational substrate upon which dynamic resilience capabilities can be built and sustained—particularly under the volatile conditions characteristic of cybersecurity disruption.

\subsubsection{Resilience as a Dynamic Managerial Capability}

Within management research, resilience is increasingly conceptualized as an operational and institutional capability embedded in decision-making, organizational structure, and strategic intent. Rather than a purely reactive outcome, resilience is understood as a proactive, path-dependent capacity shaped through routines, governance arrangements, and resource deployment. This shift reflects a broader move from classical contingency-based models toward perspectives grounded in dynamic capabilities, complex systems, and institutional embeddedness \autocite{lengnick2011developing, boin2013resilient}.\\

Recent scholarship frames organizational resilience as a form of dynamic capability that enables firms to sense, respond to, and even capitalize on disruption \autocite{teece2007explicating, duchek2020organizational}. Within this framework, resilience rests on microfoundations such as routines, heuristics, and cognitive schemas that shape how actors interpret uncertainty and act under pressure \autocite{hepfer2022heterogeneity}. Resilience is therefore not synonymous with robustness or risk avoidance, but reflects an adaptive orientation woven into both strategic intent and operational execution.\\

In practice, this capability is supported by a combination of managerial levers. Resource configurations such as slack capacity and redundancy enhance buffering potential \autocite{barasa2018what}, while structural dualities, most notably ambidextrous designs, support the simultaneous pursuit of stability and innovation \autocite{bhamra2011resilience}. Evaluative tools, including resilience audits and readiness diagnostics, further help organizations assess their ability to absorb and adapt to shocks \autocite{calder2021iso, burnard2011organisational}. Together, these levers reinforce one another, forming a foundation for organizational agility under sustained disruption. Yet these capabilities do not emerge in isolation; they are shaped by the broader structural and cultural contexts outlined above.\\

\subsubsection{Cybersecurity Resilience as a Strategic and Institutional Imperative}

Cybersecurity provides a particularly illustrative context in which these resilience dynamics unfold. Cyber risks are characterized by high uncertainty, rapid evolution, and interdependent vulnerabilities spanning technical, human, and organizational layers \autocite{linkov2013resilience}. Unlike traditional risk scenarios, cyber threats are often emergent, asymmetrical, and invisible until activated, rendering compliance-oriented and control-based approaches insufficient \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}. As a result, cybersecurity is increasingly reframed not as a narrow control problem, but as a dynamic resilience challenge \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience,linkov2013resilience}.\\

In this view, cyber resilience constitutes an integrated capability encompassing technological safeguards, strategic foresight, organizational agility, and institutional alignment \autocite{bjorck2015cyber, lostri2022shared}. Standards such as ISO 22301 emphasize the maintenance of core functions under stress \autocite{calder2021iso}, while more recent approaches stress adaptive processes rooted in learning and reflexive governance, i.e., governance systems capable of evaluating and modifying their own practices in response to evolving threats \autocite{hausken2020cyber}. Regulatory pressures, including those associated with Basel III for financial institutions, have further institutionalized resilience as a strategic imperative, linking it to organizational legitimacy and systemic risk management \autocite{baselcommittee2011basel, almeida2020challenges}. This has contributed to a broader governance turn, in which firms are increasingly held accountable not only for their own security posture but also for their contribution to the broader eco-systemic resilience \autocite{lostri2022shared, bjorck2015cyber, gillard2023efficient}.\\

Advances in AI, predictive analytics, and scenario modeling have been proposed as tools for anticipating cyber threats and orchestrating adaptive responses in real time \autocite{kuypers2018designing, gillard2023efficient}. These technologies support the simulation of threat vectors, visualization of attack surfaces, and rapid adjustment of defensive protocols, reinforcing the need for digital infrastructures that enable continuous sense-making, distributed coordination, and scalable adaptation \autocite{kuypers2018designing}. Viewed through the lens of resilience, cybersecurity thus shifts from static perimeter defense toward the dynamic coordination of people, resources, and information to enable anticipatory and responsive action at scale.\\

While the literature in management and cybersecurity substantially expanded resilience thinking, persistent challenges remain in translating these ideas into standardized, measurable, and operational practices. Field evidence suggests that organizations frequently equate resilience with compliance, struggle with quantification, and inconsistently institutionalize learning.
