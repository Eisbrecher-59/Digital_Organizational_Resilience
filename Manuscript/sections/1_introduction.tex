\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:Introduction}

In early 2023, engineers at Samsung inadvertently exposed proprietary source code by entering confidential information into publicly accessible generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools, prompting the company to ban the use of ChatGPT and similar systems among employees to prevent further leaks \autocite{ray2023samsung}. This incident was not an isolated anomaly. Since then, large organizations have reported a growing number of generative-AI-related data exposures and security violations \autocite{arora2025understanding}. More broadly, advanced AI systems are expected to automate and accelerate cyberattacks, lowering the entry barrier for sophisticated threats and enabling malicious actors to scale operations in ways previously constrained by human and technical resources \autocite{arif2024overview}.\\

These developments reflect a broader shift in the conditions under which organizations operate. As digital technologies have become more deeply embedded in organizational processes, coordination, and interdependence, disruptions increasingly arise not only from technical failures, but also from human practices and their interaction with technical systems \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}. Such disruptions cannot be addressed through technical safeguards or formal policies alone. They require organizations to build capabilities for adaptation and coordination across intertwined human and technical components under conditions of uncertainty \autocite{boin2013resilient}. Resilience therefore concerns the organizational ability to adjust, reorganize, and sustain operations as disturbances unfold \autocite{burnard2011organisational}.\\

Against this backdrop, resilience has become a central regulatory priority \autocite{heinimann2017infrastructure}. Crises such as COVID-19 \autocite{almeida2020challenges}, large-scale cyber incidents \autocite{dupont2019cyberresilience}, and systemic information technology failures \autocite{pumpuni2017resilience} have transformed resilience from an abstract organizational aspiration into a governance mandate \autocite{boin2013resilient}. Across sectors ranging from finance to healthcare and critical infrastructure, organizations are increasingly expected to anticipate, absorb, and adapt to disruption while maintaining operational continuity and legitimacy. This expectation is reinforced by landmark regulatory initiatives such as the European Union’s Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) and Australia’s Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018, which formalize resilience requirements in sectors exposed to digital and cyber disruption \autocite{european2022regulation, SOCI2018}. Yet practitioners have repeatedly expressed concern that most organizations are not designed to adapt quickly enough to an accelerating and unstable technological environment \autocite{hollnagel2013resilience}. A severe mismatch therefore persists between regulatory ambition and organizational capability: managers still struggle to define what resilience means for their organization, let alone how to measure and operationalize it \autocite{bjorck2015cyber}. More fundamentally, organizations increasingly face disruptions that are continuous, novel, and overlapping, whereas many organizational structures and assurance systems remain oriented toward a limited set of exceptional shocks. One consequence is that audit-driven compliance and rigid control frameworks often privilege documentation and formal assurance over the development of adaptive capacity \autocite{power1997audit, woods2015four}. They may signal preparedness, yet often do little to strengthen learning, coordination, and flexible response under persistent uncertainty.\\

These tensions are not confined to the literature; they are also visible in organizational practice. To complement the review, we draw on an exploratory qualitative study based on 18 semi-structured interviews with cybersecurity and risk professionals from large organizations in Switzerland, selected for their involvement in cyber risk management and organizational resilience issues (see Appendix \ref{appendix:C} for the full study design, sample characteristics, interview protocol, and detailed results). This material revealed six recurring observations (see Table \ref{tab:RotaResult}): (i) persistent ambiguity in defining and quantifying resilience, (ii) the dominance of regulatory pressure and symbolic compliance \autocite{power1997audit}, (iii) the persistence of siloed organizational structures, (iv) the limited use of incidents as opportunities for organizational learning, (v) the uneven integration of human and cultural factors, and (vi) the tension between regulatory demands for flexibility and organizational demands for precision. These observations reinforce a central point of this review: resilience is widely prescribed, but only unevenly enacted as an organizational capability.\\

The challenge of resilience is compounded by conceptual ambiguity in the scientific literature. As resilience has developed across disciplines and industries, its meaning has fragmented, ranging from system uptime to business continuity, regulatory posture, and security architecture, thereby undermining conceptual coherence and practical accountability \autocite{hillmann2021organizational, woods2015four}. Even where organizations adopt established resilience and risk-management practices -- such as audits, business continuity planning, incident response procedures, or post-incident reviews -- incidents and near-misses are seldom leveraged as sustained opportunities for organizational learning, leaving reflexivity, decentralization, and coordination underdeveloped \autocite{barasa2018what, francis2014metric}. A growing number of standards and guidelines now address resilience \autocite{linkov2019fundamental}, yet organizations still lack comprehensive methods to quantify, simulate, design, and implement it, creating the risk that resilience loses traction in practice despite its growing importance \autocite{kuypers2018designing}. These difficulties are compounded by the fact that resilience has been translated into organizational settings through partially divergent assumptions about stability, adaptation, control, and transformation.\\
\\
Advancing resilience in organizational settings therefore requires a clearer understanding of resilience as a socio-technical capability. To address this challenge in management research and practice, we adopt an integrative and problem-driven approach. Rather than adding another abstract definition or prescribing generic best practices, we examine why resilience repeatedly collapses into compliance, symbolic adoption, or fragmentation when organizations attempt to translate it into practice. By tracing how resilience has been conceptualized, operationalized, and enacted across disciplines -- and where these translations fail -- we shift the analytical focus from what resilience ought to mean to how it can be made observable and actionable in organizational settings.\\
